# Chapter Eleven: The Package

Tavin's mother opened the door with a needle between her fingers.

She had been repairing a carrier's cover. It lay across the floor behind her, heavy brown canvas patched with lighter squares. Brann was helping her hold a seam straight. Vessa sat by the stove rubbing oil into her stiff left hand.

Ragna had imagined asking to speak to Hedra alone. In front of the open door, it seemed a cruel little preparation. She said, “Tavin has been taken to Blackwater.”

The needle stayed in Hedra's hand.

“Taken by whom?”

“Harrow. Pell bound his hands. He was helping Fenn after a fall.”

“Is he hurt?”

“I saw the cord catch his sleeve. I didn't see an injury beyond that. He spoke to me before the cart left.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked about Fenn.”

Hedra looked over her shoulder at the room, then back at Ragna. “Where is Fenn?”

“With a healer. His wrist is hurt.”

“Come in.”

She took the needle out of the seam and pushed it into a cork. Ragna watched the needle enter the cork. She would have known what to do with shouting.

Hedra folded the canvas away from the doorway and cleared a chair. Brann rose but remained beside the stove.

“Tell it from when he was at the meeting,” she said.

Ragna began with Venn, the original dispatch and the request for a receipt. She described where Tavin stood, the movement in the doorway, the spell against the wall. At the places she had not seen clearly, she said so. When she came to Fenn falling, Hedra put a hand on the back of the chair.

“And my son?”

“He got him away from the weighing pan. He was trying to bring him through the side door. I had Fenn by then. Tavin moved to follow, and Pell bound him.”

“What did he have in his hands?”

“Nothing.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

Hedra sat. She was still wearing the thimble on her finger. She twisted it once before taking it off.

“He put those papers up,” she said.

Ragna nodded.

“You'd told him not to?”

“We had agreed to wait until after the bank meeting.”

“And you think that is why?”

“I don't know. Harrow had Venn's statement already. The copies brought people to the room.”

“Did my son hit anyone?”

“I saw him help Fenn. I saw no blow.”

Hedra looked at Brann. He lowered his eyes.

“You weren't there,” she said to him.

“No.”

“Then don't make that face at me. I know who wasn't there.”

She stood and opened a chest beside the bed. From it she took a clean shirt, stockings and an old wool coat. The coat had been mended at the elbows in a different colour. Tavin had worn it when he first came to the counting house. Ragna remembered thinking he would have to replace it before the winter.

“Will they let us see him?” Hedra asked.

“We can ask.”

“They can say no.”

“Yes.”

She put the stockings inside the shirt and folded both into the coat. Vessa fetched paper from the shelf. Hedra wrapped the bundle, tied it with canvas thread and wrote Tavin's name on the outside.

“You're not going alone,” Brann said.

Hedra stopped tying.

“Neither are you,” Vessa told him.

No one moved towards the door. Ragna looked at the two blankets folded on the guest bed. Brann and Vessa had arrived with a chest of plates less than a week ago. If they left now to avoid being found here, Hedra would see the empty space all night.

“I'll go with her,” Ragna said. “You decide what you need to do.”

Hedra finished the knot. “They may stay. There's no rule about my beds that changes because one is empty.”

Brann sat down slowly. Vessa picked up the oilcloth and wrapped it around the package so it would not get wet on the road.

They hired a place on a carrier's cart. This time Ragna paid. The money came from what she had kept for food. Hedra held the bundle in her lap and asked only once how long the journey was.

At Blackwater the gatekeeper stood inside the closed gate with a lamp. He recognised Ragna, then looked at Hedra's package.

“We are asking to see Tavin,” Ragna said. “From the counting house. And Daniel Venn.”

“No visitors tonight.”

“Are they here?”

He glanced towards the yard office. “Yes.”

“Both?”

“Yes.”

Hedra took a breath so deep it moved the bundle on her knees.

“Give him this.”

“I can't take things in without permission.”

“Then ask.”

He held the lamp higher. It lit the gate's new latch, the one Neri had fitted. The latch knew his hand now. His thumb rested on Neri's metalwork, and Ragna wondered if he remembered who had been meant to fit it.

“Wait here,” he said.

They waited. The carrier shifted in his seat and told them he had another delivery. Ragna paid him to leave the cart and come back on his way out. Hedra did not object to the expense.

A light moved across the office window. Someone inside laughed. Hedra shifted the package on her knees.

Pell came to the gate without his coat. His shirtsleeve was stained at the cuff. He looked tired and angry about being seen tired.

“They are being questioned.”

“We want to see them,” Hedra said.

“That isn't possible tonight.”

“What has my son been told?”

“He has been told the reason for his detention.”

“Tell me.”

“Obstruction.”

“Write it on a paper. His name. The place. The reason.”

Pell looked at Ragna. “Does nobody in your counting house accept a spoken answer?”

“She doesn't work in my counting house,” Ragna said.

Hedra held the package against the bars. “Clean clothes. No metal. No tools. You may open it in front of me.”

Pell took it and turned it over. The knot was awkward; he cut it with his wand rather than untie it. Hedra made a movement with her hand, then stopped. He shook out the shirt, checked the pockets and put everything back badly.

“I'll have it delivered.”

“Sign for it.”

“You are not at a shop.”

“No. At a shop I might be able to come back tomorrow and ask the same person.”

Ragna took out a sheet she had brought. She wrote that the package contained clothes and had been accepted at Blackwater for Tavin. She left space for a signature.

Pell read it. After a while he took the pen.

He did not let them see either prisoner. He would not take a letter. He did confirm both names in writing beneath the package receipt, with the words held pending inquiry. The paper was a miserable thing to bring back instead of a son, but Hedra held it as carefully as if the journey might tear it apart.

On the return cart she said, “He'll have the coat.”

Ragna did not say yes. She said, “Pell has signed for it.”

Hedra turned the paper over in the dark. “That's what we have, then.”

At the workshop, Sella had gathered the people who needed to decide what happened next. Not another public meeting. The table held the preserved papers, a fresh lamp and four bowls of broth. Neri had taken Ragna's copied dispatch and Ilva's statement to the far shelf, away from the food. The original ledger fragment remained in Sella's cash book.

Fenn had been taken to the healer's rooms. He had sent back instructions about the fund through Vessa: keep his name on the list of trustees, but let someone else count the coins while he could not. He wanted to hear any decision that changed the use of the money. He did not want visitors before morning.

There was also a bill. Sella put it under a cup until they had eaten.

Brann stood at the workbench, reading his own statement. The signature had dried days ago. He ran a finger beside it without touching the ink.

“I don't want this copied outside,” he said.

“It hasn't been,” Ragna replied.

“It went with the petition.”

“Yes. We cannot take that copy back.”

“I know. I want you to know I'm not agreeing to more.”

Sella nodded. “You don't have to.”

“I still found him. I'm not saying I didn't.”

“No one here has asked you to change it.”

He folded the sheet along its old crease. Vessa stood beside him but did not speak for him. Ragna wanted to tell him that more witnesses would make them safer. She could not prove that, and Tavin was at Blackwater.

Neri said, “Use mine. The one about what we saw.”

“It's your account,” Sella said. “Ragna saw it too.”

“Yes. I want it used.”

“With the date of observation,” Ragna added.

Neri looked at her. “I put it there.”

Ragna gave Sella the clean copy to read once more.

A carrier's wife came to ask whether the fund would pay for the man struck against the upright. His name was Dask. He had been taken home after the healer saw him, and his wife had gone to look for someone who knew why he had entered the weigh house.

“He says he saw a stool thrown,” she told Ragna.

“Before the first spell?”

“He isn't sure.”

“I saw a stool fall. I couldn't see the whole doorway.”

“He won't say it was the wizards if he doesn't know.”

“He shouldn't.”

The woman looked at her, surprised. She had come prepared to argue. She put the cloth she was carrying on the table and untied it. Inside were Dask's broken spectacles. One lens had gone; the wire at the bridge had been twisted flat.

“He earns by checking loads. He can't read a label without these.”

Sella fetched an empty envelope and slid the pieces into it. She wrote Fenn's workshop on the front, then crossed it out.

“There is another glassworker in the upper market,” she said. “We can ask.”

“What about the healer?”

Ragna opened the fund purse and showed the three coins. The woman looked at them without reaching for any.

“We haven't agreed what we can do about bills,” Ragna said. “There is no money for them now. Give us a copy. Keep the original.”

The woman put her hands in her lap. “I thought there would be money because you had a meeting.”

“So did some of us,” Sella said.

They gave her a bowl of broth and a place by the stove. She would not agree to Dask's name being added to another petition without asking him. Ragna put the account under private requests and wrote no public circulation across the top.

The stationer arrived after dark. She had closed her shop early and come without her hat. Ragna told her what Venn had said from the cart, then showed her the receipt confirming where he was held.

“I have sent word to Ruth,” she said. “I told her to speak to her mother herself. Neither of them is coming here tonight.”

Ragna nodded. “I'm glad they heard it from you.”

The stationer rubbed a hand over her forehead. “They'll have questions I can't answer.”

She sat beside Sella and asked to see the copy Venn had signed. Ragna laid it in front of her. The woman checked his signature, then put a small square of clean paper over the address on her own shop bill.

“Use the paper,” she said. “Don't send people to my counter to ask where my sister lives.”

They stayed long enough to agree three things. The record copies would be kept in more than one place. No additional private names would go into a public statement without permission. The partners would be asked to examine the wage evidence in a workshop, where the people whose earnings were involved could answer questions.

“Not in the court,” Sella said. “If people gather there after tonight, they will bring wands again.”

Ilva arrived as they were separating the bundles. She saw the empty chair where Tavin had sat and did not ask the question Ragna had expected.

“Is he at Blackwater?”

“Pell has confirmed it.”

Ilva put a hand on the back of the chair. “Merrok has told the partners there was violence at an unlawful gathering.”

“He wasn't there,” Neri said. “Tell them what we saw.”

Ilva looked at the piles of paper. “Issa still intends to examine the balances. Halrik arrived this evening.”

“Here,” Sella said. “If they want to ask whether I received money, they may come to the table where I counted it.”

“I can ask.”

“Ask.”

Ilva stood a moment longer, then took the request. She left the chair untouched when she went out.

Ragna helped Sella put the tools back on the bench. She had to move Tavin's fair copy of Neri's statement first. He had ruled the bottom line with such care that the paper held a faint dent beneath the ink. Ragna placed it inside the wrapper without smoothing the dent away.
