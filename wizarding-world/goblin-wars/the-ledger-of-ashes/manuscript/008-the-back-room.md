# Chapter Eight: The Back Room

Daniel Venn's mother wanted her own chair.

It stood beside the window above the baker's shop, with a cushion held together by twenty years of mending. The window looked into a wall. Ragna could see no reason to prefer it to any other window, but Mrs Venn rested one hand on the arm of the chair as if she expected someone to steal it while they spoke.

“My daughter has three chairs,” she said. “All too low. If you put me in one of them, she'll spend half her day pulling me out.”

Venn stood beside the packing case. “I'll send this after you.”

“You'll forget.”

“I won't forget a chair.”

“You forgot the winter curtains.”

“That was six years ago.”

“They were winter curtains six years ago as well.”

Sella looked at the doorway. “It will fit if the arms come off.”

“They don't,” Mrs Venn said.

“They do. I can see the joint.”

The old woman examined her. “Can you put them back on?”

“Yes.”

“Then you may.”

Ragna stepped aside while Sella crouched to examine the pegs. They had come to discuss a statement. Now the chair had to get downstairs.

Venn's sister, Ruth, arrived with a hired cart and no patience for the stairs. She was a witch of about fifty, broad through the shoulders, with her hair escaping its pins. She kissed her mother and asked whether she had packed her medicines.

“I have packed what I take.”

“That isn't always the same thing.”

Ruth checked the packet without waiting for permission. Ragna moved towards the little shelf by the door, uncertain whether to withdraw. Mrs Venn saw her.

“You're the one who knows about the iron.”

“I know some of it.”

“Daniel tells me he put it in the wrong place.”

“He changed the destination in the record.”

“Did the people who owned it say he could?”

“No.”

Mrs Venn looked at her son. He was wrapping plates in paper, holding each one as if it might break from the pressure of his fingers.

“You let me go on thinking you were in trouble for finding it out.”

“I said I'd changed an instruction.”

“You said it very quietly.”

Ruth took the plates from him and packed them herself.

“I am going to Ruth's because the stairs are too much,” Mrs Venn said to Ragna. “And because I don't want the gentleman who asked after me coming back. I'm not going because I have agreed to anything about the iron.”

“You don't have to.”

“Good.”

Sella knocked the first peg free. The chair arm came off cleanly. Mrs Venn watched her with a professional interest that made Ragna wonder what work she had done when she could move without help.

“Don't lose the little wedge,” she said. “It's been there since my husband had it repaired.”

Sella put it into an envelope and wrote on the front. Ruth said she would keep it with the chair, and her mother said she might as well, since none of them appeared to have a better system.

They took the chair down first. The aunt from the paper shop came with them and used her wand to keep its weight clear of the narrow stair. Sella guided the exposed joint past the banister. Then Ruth brought her mother down in a plain kitchen chair, the floating seat moving so steadily that the old woman continued a complaint about the baker's habit of burning the first batch.

On the pavement, Ruth gave Venn a sheet folded around an address.

“You know it. Write it down anyway,” she said. “You'll keep too much in your head and lose something.”

“I may come in a few days.”

“Send word before you arrive. We have one back room. Mother has taken it.”

Mrs Venn reached from the cart and caught his sleeve.

“Say it so they hear you,” she told him.

He bent towards her.

“I mean what you did. Don't make them ask three times.”

When the cart had gone, he remained on the pavement. Ragna let him watch until the wheels vanished around the corner.

“She could have stayed here,” he said. “They haven't done anything.”

“You can ask her to come back,” Sella said.

He looked at her, then shook his head.

Back in the empty room, he made tea in three cups that had not been packed. He had forgotten to leave spoons. They stirred it with the end of his penknife, rinsing it between cups.

“I'll speak to the officer considering your petition,” he said.

“We haven't been given a name. Whom do you intend to speak to?”

He put his cup down. “I'll put my statement through the receiving office, under my own name. They will have to enter it.”

“They will enter it,” Ragna said. “I can't promise they'll read it.”

He sat for a moment, then took the dispatch duplicate from inside his coat. He had wrapped it in an old envelope and written a date on the outside.

“I want it copied again. With a witness who does not work for the bank.”

Sella said, “Fenn.”

“Does he read?”

“He reads.”

“Then I want him to see the original before I lodge my statement. And I agree to people being told what it says. Not my mother's address.”

“It isn't in the statement.”

“Nor which baker.”

“Nor which baker,” she said.

They met Fenn at the weigh house after noon. He had brought the receipts for the flour and fuel, and two small panes of glass wrapped in cloth. He had found a spare piece that would fit Edda's lantern. He said he had not come for thanks; he wanted her to stop carrying it past his workshop with the crack facing him.

He read the dispatch duplicate and both copies. He asked Venn to point to the altered destination. Then he asked whether the initials were his, and made him say yes in front of the others.

“If they say it was copied wrongly,” Fenn said, “I can answer that part. If they say you were told something different, that's your part.”

Venn signed a short permission for the dispatch details to be included in the renewed request. He agreed that his name could be given as its witness when the statement was lodged. He did not agree to have the paper displayed at the street door with his name on it. Ragna wrote the distinction down and read it to him.

Sella rubbed at a dried spot of tea on the table while Ragna finished.

By agreement, Venn left with his original and one witnessed copy of the statement. Fenn kept the other comparison record in his own workshop, away from the counting house.

The Ministry answer arrived in the afternoon.

It had no officer's name at the top. The signature at the bottom belonged to Harrow.

Ragna read it standing in the passage outside the counting room. The existing inquiry already covered the matters raised, it said. Separate inspection of the records would prejudice that inquiry. Witnesses could be called as required. There would be no independent hearing while the investigation into missing materials remained open.

Missing materials. Not redirected materials, not unpaid materials. She read the phrase twice.

Tavin came out to find her.

“Is that the answer?”

She gave it to him.

“It's his own inquiry.”

“Yes.”

“So we ask him if he did something wrong, and he tells us he's looking into it.”

“We asked the Ministry. The Ministry sent it to him.”

Tavin read the page again. “At least put his name at the top.”

The bank's answer waited on her desk. It was shorter and, for a moment, more useful. Issa had received the request for a reserve advance. She would examine verified balances with Halrik when he arrived. They required the wage books, withdrawal slips, supporting household records, and a list separating agreed from disputed sums. No payment was promised.

Ragna dropped into the chair. They had most of those records already.

Then she saw the third letter.

Merrok had put her on temporary suspension from duties involving certification. She was to retain no authority to approve payments or to represent provisional balances as bank accounts. Her ordinary wages would continue for three days pending review. There was no accusation she could answer, only a phrase about safeguarding confidence during an inquiry.

Tavin stood very still beside the desk.

“Does that mean you have to go?”

“It means I cannot sign for the bank.”

“You haven't been signing for the bank.”

“I have been signing things he doesn't like.”

She folded the suspension letter. The packets of wage slips sat under their stones. Her first instinct was to put them in her bag. They belonged to the bank's files, though the money described in them belonged to other people. Taking the originals would give Merrok a simple allegation to put beside the complicated ones she was making.

“Call the senior clerk,” she told Tavin.

“He'll tell Merrok.”

“Yes. He should know what we do.”

The senior clerk, Arven, came from the back office. He had worked with Ragna for eight years and had never once asked her to eat with his family. She had never asked him. Now he looked at the letter and sat down without being offered a chair.

“They should have told me first,” he said.

“I'm asking you to hold the original slips. We need certified copies for the partners.”

“I cannot certify a balance that hasn't been closed.”

“I'm asking you to certify that the copies agree with these papers. Not that the household owes the amount.”

He picked up a packet and checked the first withdrawal against the copy. It took him a long time. Ragna waited, her hands in her lap.

“What do they say is disputed?” he asked.

She showed him the separate headings.

He read through three pages. Tavin shifted his weight, then stood still again.

“I will compare them,” Arven said. “I will not give you the originals. I want that understood.”

He made them wait while he fetched his own ink. For an hour the room contained nothing but the sound of pages being turned and Arven's occasional request that somebody stop standing in the light. He signed the copies in the presence of two clerks. Ragna put them in a new wrapper labelled as copies of working records, not certified balances.

She left the original packets on the desk. The key stayed in the lock until Arven had checked the drawer and taken charge of it. Then she put her empty hand in her pocket and walked out with Tavin.

“You can stay,” she said at the door.

“I finish at four.”

“It is half past.”

“Then I can go.”

She let him hold the door for her.

At Sella's workshop, they spread the papers on the cleared table. Neri had been making a new pin for the warning bell. She put the tools away and read the suspension letter first.

“How much did you put in the fund?” she asked.

“Enough.”

“That isn't a number.”

Ragna gave her the amount. Neri looked at Sella, who shook her head once.

“The gift stays a gift,” Sella said. “But you need feeding like anyone else.”

“I have rent money.”

“Good. Don't eat it.”

She set out four bowls. Bread thickened the thin soup. Ragna sat on the rocking stool, put one boot against its short leg, and read Issa's letter aloud while the others ate.

“People should hear that,” Tavin said when she finished. “About the partners. About Harrow.”

“Bring the letters tomorrow,” Sella said. “They can hear the words.”

“Will you bring the other papers?” he asked Ragna.

“The consented copies. Not every household account.”

“If the partners have them, why not the meeting?”

“Because one household has an advance for a funeral and another has a debt to a sister they are not speaking to. Neither belongs on a wall.”

“Then leave that part off.”

“Yes. That is what we need to do.”

Neri wiped her bowl with the heel of the bread. “And put what we know about the rail in a separate statement. I don't want someone reading a sum and deciding it proves what happened to my father.”

Ragna took a clean sheet. Neri dictated what they had seen: the beam, the bolts, the empty sockets, the rope. At each place Ragna had to ask where an object stood in relation to another. Neri fetched a pencil and corrected the drawing again. She drew the rope across the entrance and marked the place they had stood behind it.

Tavin began to speak, then shut his mouth. Ragna gave him the finished sheet.

“Could you make a fair copy of this for Neri to check?”

He read it before he answered. “Yes.”

Outside, the warning bell sounded. It had caught the edge of a deliveryman's hat. Neri pushed back her bowl and went to free him, complaining about the angle he had approached from. Sella called after her that they wanted the man to return next week, and Ragna held the new drawing still while Tavin ruled a straight line beneath the words they could each stand behind.
