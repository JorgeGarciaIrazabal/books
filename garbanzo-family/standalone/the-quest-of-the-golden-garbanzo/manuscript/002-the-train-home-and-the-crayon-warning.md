# Chapter 2: The Train Home and the Crayon Warning

By eight o'clock Tuesday morning, the Manhattan train felt like a rolling tin can stuffed with winter coats, wet umbrellas, and sleepy grown-ups staring at their phones.

Jorge squeezed onto a bench seat with his backpack on his knees. Outside the foggy window, New Jersey backyards blurred into gray factory roofs, then dropped underground into the dark river tunnel toward the city.

At his office twenty floors above the street, nothing felt calm or golden.

Four giant computer monitors hummed on his desk like angry bees. An overnight data job had crashed at three in the morning. Red warning boxes flashed across two screens while half a dozen team messages pinged every ten seconds.

"Jorge, got a minute?" called his teammate Sam, rolling over in a squeaky swivel chair. "The morning reports for the big bosses are jammed again. Also, did you see the new AI tool demo downstairs? That thing wrote a whole cleanup script in twelve seconds flat."

Jorge nodded and leaned over his keyboard. All year, fast new AI programs had been sweeping through the building like a sudden autumn wind. Every week a new tool did twice as much work in half the time. He loved playing with new tech at home on rainy Sundays, yet here on the twentieth floor, it made the air feel tight. Nobody knew which jobs would stay around and which teams would get reshuffled next.

For the next five hours, Jorge chased broken data pipes, fixed three tangled reports, answered frantic calls, and swallowed lukewarm takeout noodles at his keyboard because nobody had time to step away.

By four o'clock, the red boxes finally turned green. His shoulders ached. His eyes burned from orange glare.

Down the hall, two senior engineers stood by the coffee machine talking in low voices.

"You hear the rumor?" one whispered as Jorge filled his mug. "March bonuses might be huge this coming spring. Nobody with half a brain leaves before March."

"Right?" the other chuckled. "Wait one more bonus. Then maybe next year too, just for safety. Why give up easy money?"

Wait one more year.

Jorge walked back to his desk with a knot in his stomach. That voice sounded dangerously reasonable. Why walk away now? Why leave a big paycheck, clear titles, and fancy office snacks to move halfway around the world where nobody even knew what a senior data engineer did? Maybe Marissa would worry if stocks dipped. Maybe two more years of trains was the smart, safe choice.

He missed Clara's bedtime story by forty minutes.

When he finally dragged his tired boots through the New Jersey front door at nine at night, the house smelled like warm vanilla. Only the hall light glowed upstairs.

Jorge tiptoed down the hallway toward his small home office to drop his laptop bag.

He froze in the doorway.

Someone had turned his dark computer screen into a messy art gallery. Taped squarely across the dead center of his main monitor was a bright sheet of drawing paper covered in bold blue, yellow, and green wax crayon.

In the picture, a big bright yellow sun smiled from the corner. Green grass covered the bottom. In the middle stood a stick-figure Dad holding a yellow tennis ball in one hand and a wrench in the other, wearing an enormous crayon grin. Behind him stood zero computers, zero red warning boxes, and zero skyscrapers.

Across the bottom, printed carefully in thick crooked six-year-old capital letters, ran six words:

`STOP LETTING WORK EAT TUESDAYS.`

Jorge stood very still in the quiet hallway. His throat felt tight and silly all at once.

Wait two more years? At six, every Tuesday was full of lost teeth, loose shoelaces, messy paint, and giant cardboard castles in the living room. Two more "safe" years meant losing a hundred more Tuesdays while riding a dark train home after bedtime.

Down the hall, Marissa appeared in the kitchen doorway wearing her flannel bathrobe, holding out a mug of sleepytime tea. Marissa didn't say a word. She just handed him the mug.

"She drew that right after soccer practice," Marissa murmured softly. "Used three pieces of masking tape so you wouldn't miss it."

Jorge gently peeled the crayon picture off his monitor, folded it carefully once along the edge, and tucked it straight into the inside pocket of his warm coat.

"I'm done eating Tuesdays," he said quietly.

Behind Marissa, sitting on the dark kitchen counter by the toaster, the little copper pot gave a soft musical hum. Inside, beneath the half-open lid, the Golden Garbanzo blinked back to life with one tiny, stubborn speck of warm orange light.
