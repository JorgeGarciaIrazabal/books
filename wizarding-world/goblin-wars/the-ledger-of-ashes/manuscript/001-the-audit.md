# Chapter One: The Audit

The first body arrived with the morning accounts.

It lay on a handcart beneath a wool blanket, its feet sticking out where the cloth had been pulled over its face. One boot was polished. The other had a split sole, and each time the porter negotiated a bend in the passage, the sole opened and shut as if the dead man were trying to speak.

Ragna let the cart pass before she went into the counting room. The porter was a goblin she recognised from the lower furnaces. Soot had settled in the creases around his eyes.

“Who is it?” she asked.

“Dorrik.”

She had seen him two nights ago, arguing over the price of onions in the market. “What happened?”

“He fell.”

The porter said it with the careful patience of someone who had given the same answer to several people already. He moved on. Ragna watched the boot open and shut until the cart vanished around the corner.

Inside, the counting room smelled of hot metal and wet coats. Six clerks sat at their desks beneath lamps that burned without oil. Their pens moved in the quick, neat strokes of people who wanted their work finished before anyone important arrived.

Ragna hung her coat and took the leather pouch from her belt. The key inside was cold against her palm. She unlocked her desk, removed the previous day's balance, and laid it beside a clean sheet of parchment. It was the last morning of the quarter, and the accounts had to close before noon. There were families whose wages depended on the figures agreeing.

Her apprentice, Tavin, slipped into the chair opposite her. His hair had been cut short in patches, as though someone had attempted the job in poor light.

“Your mother did that?” Ragna asked.

“She was angry.”

“I can see that. What did you do?”

“Asked her to stop.”

He gave her the grin he used when he hoped to escape a question, and she let him. He was nineteen and had joined the counting house six months earlier. He could add a column twice as fast as she could, and he still believed the people who signed the accounts read them.

Ragna gave him the wage books. “Check the furnace deductions. Every one.”

“Again?”

“The numbers changed between Tuesday and yesterday.”

“I checked yesterday.”

“And today we'll know whether you were right.”

Tavin opened the first book with an exaggerated sigh. The metal clasp had been bent back and forth so often that the hinge was white. It would break soon. Ragna made a note to have it repaired, then crossed the note out. Repairs came from the counting room allowance, and the allowance was already spent.

At the far end of the room, the outer door opened. Conversation ceased. Ragna heard a lamp hiss.

A wizard entered in a dark travelling cloak, followed by two goblins from the bank's upper offices. The wizard had a long, narrow face and the expression of a man who had been kept waiting for something he considered his. He carried no case. His wand was tucked into a silver clip at his wrist.

Ragna stood. She knew the two goblins, though not well: Merrok, who managed the London accounts, and Ilva, a senior examiner. Merrok had a way of holding his hands behind his back that made his fingers look broken. Ilva met Ragna's eyes and looked away.

“This is Mr Harrow,” Merrok said. “He has authority from the Ministry to examine the special accounts.”

“We have no special accounts,” Ragna said.

Harrow took in the desks, the clerks, the books. “Then I imagine the examination will be brief.”

Tavin was still seated. Ragna touched the edge of his desk and he stood so quickly that his chair scraped the floor.

Merrok drew a folded paper from his coat. It bore a red seal. “The order is for the iron ledger.”

Ragna did not reach for it. “The iron ledger belongs to the signatories.”

“It belongs to the bank.”

“The bank holds it for them.”

“Those are not the instructions I have.”

She looked at Ilva. The examiner's face remained still. There was a small burn on the cuff of her jacket, a black circle no wider than a knuckle. Ragna remembered her at a supper three years ago, laughing with a glass in her hand. She had not expected help from her then, either.

Harrow stepped closer. “The Ministry is investigating the movement of restricted metal. We have reason to believe the ledger records certain transactions.”

“What metal?”

“That is part of the investigation.”

“And which transactions?”

“If you produce the ledger, we can establish that.”

Ragna held out her hand. Merrok gave her the order. The paper was thick and expensive, the Ministry seal pressed deep enough to show through. She read it twice, more slowly the second time. The order named the ledger and required the bank to surrender it. At the bottom were two signatures, one from the Ministry and one from the bank.

She knew the second hand. She had balanced its owner's private accounts for four years.

“The signatories haven't been consulted,” she said.

“They needn't be,” said Merrok.

“They paid to keep the record under seal.”

“They paid the bank. The bank has made a decision.”

One of the clerks shifted behind her. Ragna folded the paper carefully along its existing crease. Dorrik's name was in the ledger. So were the names of three hundred others: craftsmen who pooled their earnings to buy metal and tools, widows who held shares for children too young to sign, a dozen small workshops whose debts were recorded beside their savings. The record could tell Harrow who had bought a quantity of iron. It could also tell him who owed money, who had fallen ill, who had quietly sent wages to a brother in hiding.

“What happens to the quarter's wages?” she asked.

Harrow looked at her as if she had changed the subject.

“We close at noon,” Ragna said. “The ledger carries the signed balances. The wage books show what each workshop earned, but not what it has already drawn. Without the balances we cannot authorise payment.”

Merrok's mouth tightened. “Make a copy of the totals.”

“That will take time.”

“Then begin.”

She could have refused. The thought came to her cleanly and passed. Merrok had signed the order; Harrow had his wand; every clerk in the room was listening. A refusal would cost her the ledger and her position before she had learned what they wanted from either. It might cost the wages too.

“Two hours,” she said.

“One,” said Harrow.

She looked at the clock. “One hour, then.”

Merrok gave her the key to the strongroom. It was attached to a chain which he unwound from his wrist with a little ceremony. Ragna took it, aware of Tavin watching her. She had taught him that an account was a promise to the person whose name it carried. Now she was going to open that account for a stranger.

“Come with me,” she told him.

The strongroom was across the passage and down a short flight of steps. Ilva took a lamp from its bracket and followed them, her shoes making no sound on the stone. The door was iron-bound oak, its lock shaped like a closed eye. Ragna put the key in and turned it twice. The eye opened; she felt the familiar pressure against her thoughts, the lock searching for a name it knew. Then the bolts drew back.

The room beyond was cool and dry. Chests lined the walls. At the centre stood a stone table, and on it the iron ledger, its cover dark and dull as river water. It was too large for one goblin to carry comfortably, which was one reason it had survived so many attempts to move it.

Tavin put his hands beneath the book. Ragna stopped him.

“Bring the stool,” she said.

He fetched it from the corner. She sat and opened the ledger to the wages, finding the page by touch before Ilva brought the lamp closer. Columns filled the paper in a dozen hands. Some entries had been amended, the old figures struck through but still legible. A good account showed its corrections.

Ragna set a blank sheet over the opposite page. Tavin opened his ink.

“Read them to me,” he said.

She read the first ten totals. He wrote quickly. At the eleventh she paused.

Dorrik's workshop had been charged for forty bars of iron. The entry was in her own hand, made a fortnight ago from a delivery note brought up from the furnaces. Beneath it was a second charge for the same amount, entered yesterday. The hand was unfamiliar.

“Forty bars,” she said. “Twice.”

Tavin leaned over the page. “That's wrong. I checked the deductions.”

“When?”

“Yesterday morning. It wasn't there.”

Ilva came to the table. “Is there a problem?”

“A duplicated entry.” Ragna kept her finger on the figures. “It changes the balance.”

“Correct it on the copy.”

“I need the delivery note.”

“You don't have time.”

“Then I won't know which entry is right.”

Ilva glanced toward the open door. “Write down the total as it stands. You can make an adjustment next quarter.”

Dorrik's widow would have to wait three months for the money. Perhaps longer, if the ledger was still in Ministry hands. Ragna looked at the entry again. It had been made in black ink, but the iron nib had caught on a rough patch of paper. Under the final stroke, a faint red line showed through.

Someone had written over something else.

She put the tip of her nail beneath the page. A corner lifted, just enough to show the edge of another sheet. Two pages had been stuck together.

Ilva put her hand on the book. “Leave it.”

Ragna looked up. “You knew.”

“I know we have less than an hour.”

Tavin's pen hovered over the parchment. “What should I write?”

For a moment nobody answered. From above came the muted drag of furniture across the counting room floor. Harrow had grown tired of waiting. Or he had started searching without the ledger.

Ragna took the small knife from her belt. Ilva caught her wrist.

“Think carefully,” Ilva said.

Ragna did. She thought of Dorrik's split boot and the porter who had said he fell. She thought of the extra charge, which would take nearly all the wages owed to his workshop. None of it proved a murder, or even a theft. It was enough to make her want to see the page.

She pulled her wrist free and slid the knife between the sheets. The adhesive crackled. Tavin held his breath as she worked down the margin. When the pages came apart, a narrow strip of parchment tore away and clung to the back of the upper sheet.

There was a list underneath. Not names this time, but quantities and destinations, each marked with a date. Beside the most recent entry was a familiar signature: Merrok's.

The door at the top of the stairs opened.

Ilva swore quietly. She snatched the loose strip from Ragna's hand, folded it into her palm and closed the ledger.

“Carry it up,” she said.

Ragna stared at her.

“Now.”

Footsteps descended. Tavin lifted one end of the book; Ragna took the other. Harrow appeared at the bottom of the stairs just as they reached the door. His eyes went to the knife in Ragna's hand, then to Ilva.

“Is there a difficulty?”

“No,” Ilva said. “The ledger is ready.”

Harrow held out his hand for the knife. Ragna gave it to him, handle first. He examined the blade and wiped it on his cuff before returning it.

“Bring the book.”

They carried it upstairs. The clerks had been moved away from their desks and stood together by the wall. Merrok watched Ragna as she set the ledger down in front of the wizard. She could not tell whether he knew what she had seen.

Harrow opened the cover. The pages turned of their own accord, one after another, until they stopped at the wages. He inspected the figures, then the damp sheet in Tavin's hand.

“You haven't finished.”

“No,” Ragna said.

“Pity.” He closed the book. “You'll have to make other arrangements.”

He placed two fingers on the cover, and the ledger shrank until it was small enough to slip into his cloak. Ragna felt a sharp, unreasonable need to ask whether he had folded any of the pages. She kept her mouth shut.

Merrok told the clerks to return to work. They went back to their desks without speaking. Harrow left with him; Ilva followed last. At the door she stopped and held out her hand to Ragna.

“The strongroom key.”

Ragna gave it to her. Ilva's fingers closed around it, and something small and dry pressed into Ragna's palm.

When the door had shut, Ragna opened her hand beneath the desk. The torn strip lay across her skin. It held only part of a line: a date, the number forty, and the end of a place name.

She knew the place. It was Blackwater shaft, where Dorrik had fallen.
