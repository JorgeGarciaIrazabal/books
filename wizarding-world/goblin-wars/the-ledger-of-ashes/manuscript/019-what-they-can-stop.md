# Chapter Nineteen: What They Can Stop

That afternoon, Halrik arrived at the workshop without his coat. The rain had soaked the shoulders of his shirt, and he had brought the wrong boot on his left foot.

“I put it on in the dark,” he said when Sella looked at it. “My brother has the other one. He was less amused than I was.”

He accepted a cloth for his hair and read Borr's statement at the bench. Issa stood beside him. She had brought the Ministry order and laid it face up for everyone to see.

“Arven has the originals,” she said. “He has been told to prepare them for collection.”

“Has he?” Ragna asked.

“He has boxed them and made a list of the contents. He wants two people to witness the count.”

Sella put down the piece of brass she had been holding for Neri. “At the counting house?”

“At the bank house. The counting room is too small for the boxes.”

Neri sat beside the stove, her foot supported on a stool. She had come home that morning in a hired cart. The healer had given her instructions that Sella had written out and fastened above the bench. Neri had already moved the paper so she did not have to look at it.

“Will they let us keep copies?” she asked.

“The order says copies made for the examination may remain with the bank.”

“That doesn't say the workshops can have them.”

“No,” Issa said.

Halrik read Borr's last correction and turned the sheet over. “He saw the twenty arrive at Thorn. He counted with a clerk. He hasn't seen the entry.”

“We have Venn's dispatch,” Ragna said.

“And the household's stock book.” He rubbed a hand across his wet hair. “I will accept that the twenty are disputed. The forty charged a second time is still a different question.”

“How much can you pay?” Sella asked.

They worked it out on the back of an old delivery bill. It took longer than Ragna expected because Halrik insisted on checking the rate against the original note, and Issa insisted on checking the withdrawals against Arven's certified copies. The result was a positive balance. The partners agreed to advance half now and retain the rest against the unresolved duplicate charge. Sella could pay part of the wages that had waited since the quarter closed, but the next order of moulding sand would still have to wait.

“Today?” Sella asked.

“After we have the original slips,” Issa said. “At the bank house.”

“And the remaining charge?”

“Still open.”

Sella nodded once. She did not say thank you. She went to the drawer and took out the tally sticks, checking each bundle against the list she had made when she thought the wait might last two days.

Ragna took Issa aside while Halrik helped Neri lower her foot from the stool.

“What will you do with the order?”

“I will obey it unless the partners agree to challenge it.”

“Merrok won't.”

“Vale is the Ministry officer assigned to supervise the collection. She can suspend a transfer while an incident is examined, and ask for the detention records. I have sent her our objection. She has not promised to intervene. Merrok says refusing will close every account we hold in London.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I believe he can make it difficult. That's different.” Issa looked towards Sella. “He also says the people at the weigh house have threatened bank property.”

“Who?”

“He didn't give a name.”

“Then ask him for one.”

“I have. He said you would not give him the names of the people who put up the notices.”

Ragna thought of Tavin insisting there had been no one above him. Merrok was making a conspiracy out of a decision she had been angry with Tavin for taking alone.

“Tell him the notices were the room's words,” she said. “The timing was Tavin's.”

Issa folded the order. “Will you tell the room that?”

“Yes.”

The meeting was held at the weigh house, though the broken lamp bracket had not been replaced. The side door was open, and the baskets that had blocked it were stacked in a neighbour's yard. Fenn came on foot with Vessa, his right hand held in a sling. He had tried to lift a sheet of glass that morning and had dropped it. He reported this before anyone could ask.

“I won't try again tomorrow,” he said.

“Will you have work?” Edda asked.

“I have work. I don't know who will do it.”

Brelk had brought his son. The boy sat on a stool near the door, wrapped in a blanket, with a cup of water between his knees. His cough was less frequent than before, but each bout took Brelk's attention away from the room.

Ragna read the Ministry order. She did not read the names on the private records. She told them Issa and Halrik could pay part of Sella's balance that afternoon, and that the remaining charge was still disputed.

“That will put food on a table,” Hirk said. “It won't keep my records out of a box.”

“No,” Ragna agreed.

A fitter from the furnace wanted to take the boxes before Arven could give them up. Another said they should burn the ledgers, since nobody could seize ashes.

“My wages are in them,” Edda said.

“So are mine,” the fitter replied. “I'd rather lose the figures than have them carry my aunt's name to the court.”

“Ask your aunt,” Vessa said.

The fitter turned to her. “She's dead.”

“Then ask the people still on the page.”

The room went quiet. Vessa looked as if she wanted to take the words back. The fitter sat down before she could.

Ragna put the order on the table. “We can ask Arven to separate the records by household. We can make certified copies for the people named in them. It will take more than a morning.”

“They come at noon,” Fenn said.

“Yes.”

“Then what do we do at noon?”

Brann had not stood at a public meeting since Tavin was taken. He rose now with his cap in his hands. His sister shifted to make space but did not touch him.

“The lift at Blackwater needs the chain tested before the Ministry moves a load,” he said. “Vessa's team does it. If they don't test, the foreman has to sign the lift fit himself.”

“Can he?” someone asked.

“He can put his name down. Whether he will is another matter.”

Vessa looked at him. “You didn't ask me.”

“I know.”

“I won't test it. But the other two decide for themselves.”

One of those workers, a woman called Tola, stood near the door. She had missed a day's wages after refusing an attendance report. She said she would not test the chain. The other tester was not at the meeting. Vessa would ask him before the morning shift.

Neri wanted to go to Blackwater. Sella told her she could not walk to the door, let alone the works. Neri argued for a while and then asked for a slate. She began listing the repairs scheduled for Ministry vehicles, including the work Mere had expected to do herself. Hask had already said he would not release new fittings to Thorn until Mere's cart was returned or paid for.

Hirk said she would still deliver sand to the school workshop. Brelk said he could not stop work at the forge that paid his son's room, but he could carry messages after his shift. The carters at the meeting agreed to ask the drivers hired for the bank-house transfer to refuse the load until the detainees were accounted for. They could not commit drivers who were not present; those who refused might lose their Ministry work. Edda said she would stop her furnace on the morning run if the other operator agreed; she would not shut it down with someone inside the lower chamber.

No one voted on a general strike. They wrote down what each person could withhold, whom it would affect, and when they had to decide. The Ministry could still walk through the doors. But supplies might wait at Blackwater, and the reserve boxes might stay at the bank house, if the workers and carters held to their decisions.

Ragna told them about Harrow's offer. She gave the terms as she understood them and said she had not seen the undertaking. She told them Tavin would not sign a promise he had not read, and that Venn had not been consulted.

“Would you have given him the counterfoil?” Sella asked.

Ragna could have said no. The answer would have been clean and false.

“I wanted to,” she said. “For a moment.”

Sella looked down at her hands. “So did I when you told me.”

Mere had come late, carrying the bill for the hired cart. She laid it on the table without asking anyone to pay it immediately.

“I want my cart back,” she said. “I want Neri's tools back. I also want the receipt returned when the partners have finished with it. If you put it in front of Harrow, I want to be there.”

“You may need it for your claim,” Ragna said.

“I do. You need it for yours. There is no reason one of us has to pretend otherwise.”

They agreed to make two attested copies. Mere would hold the original unless she authorised its use at the bank house. Sella gave it to her across the table, with the invoice beside it. Ragna watched the transfer and wrote the date on the wrapper.

By the time the meeting ended, the room had a list of work that would stop and a second list of work that would continue. It had no promise that the Ministry would wait. Fenn took the first list to Vessa to check. Brelk took the second, so the school would know its repairs could still be made.

At the bank house Arven had opened every box. He had laid the books out in order and marked the private household files with strips of white cloth. Issa and Halrik watched while Sella checked the totals on her balance. Ragna checked the arithmetic beside her. Neither partner asked Sella to sign a release.

The clerk counted the money twice. Sella counted it a third time and put it into two envelopes. One went to the workers who had accepted partial pay. The other she held for the fitter who had refused to lend his labour and had found work elsewhere. She sent Brelk to tell him it was ready.

Ragna carried no envelope home. The three paid days of her suspension had ended without a decision from Merrok, and she had spent the money for her reading lenses on food, fares and a healer's visit. When she told Sella she might have to ask the fund for coal, Sella wrote her name on the private list without commenting on it.

“Who will count it?” Ragna asked.

“Someone who isn't applying for it.”

“Fair.”

“Annoying, isn't it?”

Ragna laughed. It hurt her jaw. She touched the cut and found the edge of a scab.

Outside the bank house, a delivery cart waited with its horse untethered from the shafts. The driver sat on the pavement eating bread. He said he had been told there might be no one to test the lift in the morning and saw no reason to make the horse stand harnessed all night.

“Who told you?” Ragna asked.

“Someone at the furnace.”

“Was it a decision?”

“Sounded like one.” He tore off another piece of bread. “If they change their minds, they can find me at the inn.”

Ragna looked at the shafts resting on the ground. Tomorrow they would have to find the drivers before rumour made more promises in their names.
