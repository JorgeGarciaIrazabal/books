# 7. Boxes, Boxes

The shed door stuck. Aunt June pushed it with her shoulder, and it opened with a wooden groan.

Inside, everything smelled of wood, earth, and the sharp green smell of tomato leaves.

Dad hooked the door open. Daylight fell across a row of watering cans and a very large number of boxes.

There were boxes on shelves. Boxes on the floor. Boxes leaning against other boxes as if they were tired.

Mila had expected to see one box with yellow wool around it.

She could see at least six.

Some were deep. Several were broad and flat, like the one Mrs. Bell had described.

“Oh,” Noah said.

Aunt June set down her basket. “We'll start with the ones we can reach.”

Mila stepped around a watering can. On a low shelf, a wide cardboard box had one word written in thick black letters.

STARS.

She looked at Dad's bag.

Her stars were still inside it.

“Could someone else have made stars?” Mila asked.

“Certainly,” said Aunt June.

She lifted the box down, untied its yellow wool, and opened the lid.

Inside were small empty plant pots, stacked in rows.

Noah picked up one of the pots and looked through the hole in its bottom.

“Very small stars.”

Mila laughed despite herself.

“We use boxes again,” Aunt June said. “That one held paper stars for the winter fair. I should have crossed the old word out.”

Mila put a hand against the cardboard. The word was clear and dark. It looked like a promise about what was inside.

But it wasn't.

June set the open box near the door. Dad checked behind it.

Only more pots.

At the back of the shed, three wide cartons were stacked beneath a heavy wooden tray. Another flat box stood against the wall beside them.

Mila could see yellow ties. She couldn't see all the writing.

“We'll need to move the tray first,” Dad said.

Mila reached toward the nearest corner.

“That's a two-grown-up lift,” Aunt June said. “You can keep the doorway clear for us.”

Mila moved the watering cans outside, one at a time. Noah carried the smaller ones. They lined them up beneath the plum tree.

Footsteps crunched on the path.

Mr. Moss came around the corner pulling a handcart full of green plants. A leaf had stuck to his sleeve.

“Any luck with your lantern?” he asked.

“Not yet,” Mila said. “Did you carry a big, flat box from the library?”

“Yes. Along with two smaller ones. They were waiting by the back door.”

“On the table?”

“The big one was. I slid it onto the trolley.”

Mila pictured the yellow knot catching on the rough wood.

“What was in it?”

Mr. Moss shook his head. “I didn't look inside.”

“I was taking garden supplies over,” Mr. Moss said. “The big box said TO THE GARDEN. So that's where I took it.”

“Our moon was in a big box,” Mila said.

His eyebrows rose. “Then I may owe your moon a ride home.”

“Where did you put it?” Noah asked.

“Against that wall, with the others. Before the rain.”

He pointed toward the boxes behind the wooden tray.

Aunt June looked at the stack. “We had to put a lot more in front when the shower started.”

Mr. Moss took off his coat. “Let's make some room and start opening them.”

Mila stood back as he and June lifted the heavy tray. Dad caught a loose flowerpot before it rolled.

There were more boxes behind it than Mila had seen.

Somewhere among them might be a moon.

She opened Noah's notebook and looked at the space she had left for a name.

Then she turned to a clean page.
