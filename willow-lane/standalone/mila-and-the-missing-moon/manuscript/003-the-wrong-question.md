# 3. The Wrong Question

“Why did you take our moon?” Mila asked.

Bea's hands stopped moving.

“I didn't.”

“But you wanted the table. And there's flour all the way out of the library.”

Bea looked toward the step. Her smile had gone.

“I used an empty table, Mila. If something was in my way, I would ask someone to help move it.”

The hot fizz in Mila's chest began to feel different.

Dad came back from the bakery with empty hands.

“What's happening?”

“Mila thinks I took the lantern.” Bea spoke quietly. That was somehow worse than shouting.

“We found white wheel marks,” Mila said.

“You did,” Dad said. “What else do you know?”

She wanted to say that she knew plenty. But all she could see was Bea's hand, still gripping the edge of her apron.

Noah touched Mila's sleeve.

“Can we try the white stuff now?”

Bea gave them a pinch of flour in a saucer. Dad brought two more saucers and a mug of water from the library.

With an old spoon, Noah scraped up a little dry powder from the edge of a wheel mark. Mila fetched a crumb of pale dirt from the muddy patch beside the garden gate.

Three samples. Three saucers.

They added a little water to each and stirred with separate sticks.

Bea's flour became a sticky white lump. The dirt made a gritty, cream-colored smear. So did the powder from the track.

Mila rubbed a bit between her fingers. Tiny grains scratched her skin.

“These two feel alike,” she said.

Dad peered at them. “This patch has pale clay in it. Wheels have been going through it all day.”

Mila looked at the flour again. Their trail sample didn't look or feel like Bea's sample at all.

She wished she had done this first.

<!-- PAGEBREAK -->

Bea's tray cart stood just inside her door. It had two large wheels and a handle, like a shopping trolley.

Noah checked both wheels. Their rubber edges were smooth. With his ruler, he measured how far apart they were.

Back at the library step, he measured the space between the two long, straight tracks. They were much closer together than Bea's wheels. At the turn, the lines spread into several curved marks. The little gaps kept appearing in one outside line.

Mila drew Bea's two wheels on the page beneath her notes. Then she drew the narrower pair of tracks.

They weren't the same.

Through the bakery door came the smell of warm biscuits. Mila usually liked that smell very much. Now it made her feel smaller.

“I'll wait here,” Noah said.

She shook her head. “You can come.”

Dad stayed beside the step as they crossed the yard.

Bea was putting a cloth over a bowl.

“I was wrong,” Mila said. “I said you took it before I knew. I'm sorry.”

She kept her eyes on Bea this time.

<!-- PAGEBREAK -->

Bea set the bowl down.

“Thank you for coming back to tell me.”

Mila waited. Bea didn't say that it didn't matter. Mila was glad, a little. It had mattered.

“Could I ask a different question?”

“You can.”

“Did you see a big, flat box when you brought the trays?”

Bea rubbed her chin with the back of her wrist.

“Not inside. But I passed a trolley in the yard. There was a square box on it, tied with yellow wool.”

Mila's fingers tightened around the pencil.

“Who was pushing?” Noah asked.

“I couldn't see. I was carrying my trays in front of me. Whoever it was waited while I squeezed past.”

“What kind of trolley?”

“Low and wooden. Four small wheels, I think. I was mostly watching my biscuits.”

Mila wrote it down, leaving space beneath it.

This time, she hadn't found an answer.

But she had a much better question.
