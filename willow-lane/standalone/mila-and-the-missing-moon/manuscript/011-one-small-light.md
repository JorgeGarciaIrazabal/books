# 11. One Small Light

At dusk, Dad clipped a small battery light behind the moon's paper and pressed its switch.

The moon glowed.

Mila had seen the white sheets, the paste, the bare frame, and the inside of the box. She knew how it all fitted together.

Still, she leaned closer.

The little patch made a pale star near the bottom. Below it, the strings swayed, each carrying one of Mila's cut-out stars.

Noah stood on the other side of the bar, looking through the fringe.

“Can you see me?” he asked.

“Only your nose.”

He moved his head. Now she could see one eye too.

Behind them, the library yard filled with glowing fish, houses, flowers, and things Mila couldn't name. One little boy carried a paper shape with six legs and a hat.

Dad lowered the middle of the bar into his hands.

“Ready?”

Mila took her end. Noah took his.

They went slowly across the yard. Dad carried the weight while Mila and Noah kept the ends steady.

For a few steps, Mila watched only the paving stones. She didn't want to trip now.

Then she looked up.

Lantern light slid over wet leaves. The puddle by the mint held an upside-down paper fish. Every time the child carrying it moved, the fish in the puddle wriggled.

At the bakery corner, Bea waited with a basket on her arm.

Mila felt a small tightness in her stomach. She almost looked down again.

“Would you walk beside us?” she asked.

Bea glanced at the broad moon. “Is there room?”

“On my side.”

Bea joined them. Warm biscuit smells came from her basket.

“I'm glad you found it,” she said.

“Noah's map helped,” Mila told her. “And he worked out the wheel marks.”

Across the bar, Noah looked pleased. Then he pointed ahead.

“Gatepost,” he said.

They all moved a little left.

The walk wound through the garden. Aunt June had hung small lights along the fence, and the plum tree spread dark branches over their heads.

Beside the shed, Mr. Moss stood with the plastic dinosaur in his coat pocket.

“Right destination this time?” he asked.

“Just passing through,” Mila said.

At the end of the garden, everyone stopped while the people at the back caught up. Dad rested the bar on two supports someone had set beside a bench.

Mila loosened her fingers. She hadn't noticed how tightly she was holding on.

Bea passed around small round biscuits. Mila broke hers in half and gave a piece to Dad.

Crumbs fell onto his coat.

“A useful pocket,” he said, catching one.

Noah sat beside Mila, balancing his biscuit on one knee. The moon hung in front of them, bright against the shed's dark wall.

For a little while, nobody needed to carry it or fix it or find it.

They returned to the library along Willow Lane. The real moon had come out between two thin clouds.

Mila looked up at it, then at theirs.

Theirs was lower. It also had strings.

She liked it very much.

Back in the yard, the little boy with the six-legged lantern asked why the moon had a star on its face.

“It needed a patch,” Noah said.

“Mine needs a tail.”

“Ask Mrs. Bell,” Mila told him. “She's got more paper.”

Dad and Mr. Moss carried the moon inside. This time they hung it in the library window, where everyone could see what it was.

Noah took his notebook from Mila and brushed a biscuit crumb off the cover.

“Can I finish the last page tonight?” she asked.

He nodded. “Bring it tomorrow. And the pencil.”

“I will.”

They bumped elbows because their hands were sticky.

Then Mila went home with Dad, leaving the paper moon shining in the window behind them.
