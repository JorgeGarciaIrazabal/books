# Chapter Two: The Household

By noon the counting room had acquired a queue.

Ragna could see the first three people through the half-open door. A woman held a broken lantern, though it was broad daylight above ground. Behind her a furnace worker was eating something from a cloth, slowly, to make it last. The third person was Sella, Dorrik's wife. She had brought a basket.

Tavin stood beside Ragna's desk with the unfinished copy of the balances.

“We could pay the first ten,” he said.

“And the eleventh?”

“They would have something.”

“The first ten would. Tell the others why they came too late.”

He looked down at his sheet. So much for teaching him something useful. She had made him ashamed of trying.

“Bring the withdrawal slips,” she said. “We may be able to reconstruct more of it.”

“May?”

“Yes. May.”

Sella came in without being called. She set the basket on the desk and took out a folded coat, a packet of needles, and a small blue cup with its handle wrapped in string. Finally she removed a wooden box and put everything else away.

“These are his day tallies,” she said. “The workshop ones are downstairs. I thought you'd want both.”

Ragna stood.

Sella's hair was bound back so tightly that the skin at her temples shone. One cuff was damp, and there was a crescent of pale dust on her shoulder. Whatever she had been doing when the news came, she had not changed.

“I was going to come to you,” Ragna said.

“I need his balance. There are people waiting to be paid.”

“Sella, the ledger has been taken.”

“By whom?”

“The Ministry. With the bank's agreement.”

“Does the Ministry intend to pay our people?”

“No arrangement has been made.”

Sella looked at the box, then at Tavin. “Then we'd better make one.”

She slid the lid back. Inside, tally sticks had been tied in bundles with red thread. Each stick carried a workshop mark, a date and notches showing the hours worked. Dorrik had been a fitter, but he had done the household's deliveries as well. Ragna knew the long, sloping cut he used for seven. It had irritated her for years because it looked like a damaged nine.

“I have what he earned,” she said. “What I need is the signed balance after the advances.”

“He took no advance this quarter.”

“Against his own wages?”

“Against anyone's. We sold a set of hinges to a Muggle carrier in February and kept enough from that. I can show you.”

Tavin opened his mouth, then thought better of it.

“Good,” Ragna said. “I will need to see it.”

“You can see it at our table.”

“The office closes at four.”

“Your office can do as it likes. Our men finish at three, and they expect money.”

She put the lid on the box. The blue cup showed through the top of the basket. Ragna recognised it as the one Dorrik brought to work. He had mended the handle badly, which had been a standing joke among the fitters. A man who could set a gate so true that it closed under its own weight had tied his cup together with string.

Ragna took her coat.

“Tavin, gather every slip we have. Keep them here. Nobody takes an original.”

“Are we open?” he asked.

She looked past Sella at the people in the passage.

“Tell them where I am.”

Sella picked up the basket and went first.

The family's workshop stood in a row of vaulted rooms beneath a yard near the river. Ragna had passed the yard a hundred times without entering. Above ground a carter was swearing at a wheel; below, someone was practising a tune on a pipe and missing the same note each time. The passage smelled of coal, cabbage and damp stone.

Four names stood on the brass doorplate. Dorrik's was second; Sella's came first.

Inside, six small bells hung on a wooden frame. As Ragna crossed the threshold, one gave a low, displeased note.

“Mind the sill,” Sella said.

Ragna stepped back. The loose nail that had caught her coat sank into the wood as Sella pressed it with a thumb.

“It ought to do that before you arrive,” she said. “Neri has been improving it.”

“It's meant to keep strangers out,” said a young woman from the workbench. “If you keep telling them where to step, it cannot learn.”

She was filing a thin metal tongue. The bench held pieces of hinges, a half-assembled latch and three mugs of cold tea. A boy sat beneath it, sorting rivets into a tray with ten compartments.

“This is Ragna,” Sella said. “She has come about the wages.”

The young woman put down her file. “Has she brought them?”

“No.”

“Then she can have the other stool.”

The stool rocked beneath Ragna. She moved it half a hand's breadth to the left. It rocked there too.

Neri watched her with an expression much like her father's. Dorrik had once looked at a charge of three sickles for replacing a lock and asked whether the new one could sing.

“Orren,” Sella said to the boy, “put the kettle on.”

“I sorted these already.”

“Then they'll be there when you return.”

He climbed out, keeping one hand closed. Sella caught his wrist and held it until he dropped four rivets into the right compartment.

“For your boat?” she asked.

He nodded.

“Ask.”

He went to the little stove without asking.

Ragna kept her hands flat on her knees. There ought to have been something she could say in a room like this. She had known Dorrik for sixteen years. She had never known that his son was building a boat.

“I am sorry,” she said.

Sella turned a mug upright. “So am I.”

The kettle began to shiver. Orren pressed two fingers to a round stone set beside the stove. The shivering ceased, and a thin thread of steam climbed from the spout.

“We were told he fell,” Ragna said.

“He did fall,” Neri said. “Everybody seems satisfied with that.”

“Do you know what happened before?”

Sella poured water over the leaves in the pot. “They brought him at ten. Brann brought him. There was a man with him from the bank who said Dorrik had no business in the lower shaft after the shift bell.”

“Which man?”

“I don't know. He had a red waistcoat and a paper for me to sign.”

“Did you sign it?”

“No.”

Ragna looked at Neri.

“My mother can refuse a paper without assistance,” Neri said.

“I didn't mean that.”

“People have been arriving all morning meaning things.”

Sella set the teapot down hard enough to interrupt them both. “The paper is over there.”

It lay beneath a weight on the end of the bench. Ragna lifted it. A receipt for the return of a body, it said. In smaller writing below, it acknowledged the deceased's presence outside his assigned working area. Below that was a sentence stating that the household held no claim against the carrier.

“Who was the carrier?” she asked.

“He said it meant Brann,” Sella said.

“Did Brann say that?”

“Brann said nothing.”

The porter from the morning. Ragna could still see him pushing the cart, exhausted by questions. She read the sentence again. A carrier could be a man, or a business, or the party responsible for a load. The paper did not say.

“Keep this,” she said.

“I was going to.”

“I mean, don't give it back if they ask.”

Sella poured a cup of tea and pushed it towards her. “Now the wages.”

The household books occupied a drawer beneath the bench. They were wrapped in an old apron, with a bundle of bills pressed between them. Ragna opened the cash book. Sella's hand was small and upright. There was no empty space at the end of a page; when she reached the bottom, she turned the book sideways and finished along the margin.

Ragna followed the entries from the hinge sale through coal, food, materials and the wages already paid. She checked the arithmetic twice. It was right.

“You have enough for two days,” she said.

“One,” Sella said. “We owe for the moulding sand. The bill arrived this morning.”

“I could speak to the supplier.”

“I already did. She can wait until Friday. What she cannot do is give us another load while we owe for the first.”

Neri picked up a hinge and worked it open with her thumb. “We have enough metal to finish the school order. If we finish it, we can ask for payment early.”

“Would they agree?”

“They may. If they don't, I shall have spent an afternoon doing the job I had to do anyway.”

Ragna turned another page. “These forty bars. You received them a fortnight ago?”

Sella looked at the entry. “We received twenty.”

“The delivery note says forty.”

“Then the note is wrong.”

“Dorrik brought it himself.”

Neri stopped moving the hinge.

“He brought it to you?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“And you entered forty.”

“It had been signed.”

“By whom?”

Ragna remembered the paper less clearly than she wanted to. The date, the weight, the rate. A signature beneath each of the two columns. One from the works, one from the household.

“Your father,” she said.

Neri put the hinge down. “Then he signed for iron we never had.”

Ragna waited. Sella lifted the teapot, found it empty and set it back.

“That is what you think?” she asked her daughter.

“I think she has his signature and we have twenty bars.”

“Could they have been delivered elsewhere?” Ragna asked.

“Our store is the cupboard behind you,” Sella said. “If you can find twenty bars in it that I have overlooked, you may keep one.”

Orren came back to the bench. “He went to ask about it.”

All three women turned towards him.

“About what?” Sella asked.

“The other iron. He said Neri had counted twice and she was too tiresome to be wrong twice.”

“When did he say that?”

“Yesterday. He said he'd find the man with the other cart.”

Neri looked at the floor. “I told him to take it back. The signed note. I told him if he signed for forty, we would pay for forty.”

“Why didn't you bring it to me?” Ragna asked.

“Because he said he would sort it out,” Sella said. “And because I had a houseful of people asking him about forty things a day. You knew what he was like.”

At her desk, Dorrik apologised for wet boots and argued about charges. She had not known what he carried home.

She took the torn strip from her pocket and laid it on the table.

“This came from the ledger. A page had been covered over. I don't know what the entry meant, but there were quantities and places on it.”

Sella leaned close. Neri did not touch it.

“Blackwater,” she said.

“That's what I thought.”

“It is. We have done enough of their gate fittings.”

Ragna had meant to withhold Merrok's signature until she knew more. Neri was waiting, and Sella had stopped pouring the tea.

“The page was signed by Merrok.”

“The bank man?” Sella asked.

“Yes.”

“Does that mean he ordered it?”

“I don't know.”

“Does it mean Dorrik was carrying something for him?”

“I don't know that either.”

“Then what have you brought us?”

The boy had gone very still beside the rivets. Ragna folded the strip once along its crease.

“A reason to ask for the delivery records,” she said. “And a problem with the charge against your wages. That I can prove. There are two entries for the same forty bars.”

Neri gave a short laugh with no pleasure in it. “Eighty, then. We ought to be building a bridge.”

Sella opened her hand. Ragna hesitated, then put the strip in it. She watched Sella turn it to the light, read it, and place it on the bench beside the unsigned receipt.

“We need a copy,” Sella said.

“Yes.”

“Two. One for us, one for you.”

“I'll bring paper.”

“We have paper.”

Neri took a sheet from a shelf. It was an old bill with the back unused. She cut it into three pieces and passed two to Ragna. The third she kept. They made separate copies of the fragment and compared them letter by letter. Ragna gave the original back to Sella.

“Keep it dry.”

“I make door fittings. My house has a roof.”

For the first time that day, Ragna nearly smiled.

They spent the next hour listing what was owed to each worker. When the fitters came in, Sella explained what she could pay and what she could not. One left to ask a cousin for work. Another agreed to finish the school hinges for half his usual daily payment, with the rest owed. The third said he would not lend the workshop money by working for less, and Neri told him she understood. He stayed long enough to show her where a pin on the largest hinge would bind.

Ragna wrote each agreement down. She did not ask anyone to make a speech about loyalty.

When the room was quiet again, Sella took Dorrik's coat out of the basket and hung it on the peg beside the door. She put the blue cup on the shelf. The needles went into a jar.

“Neri will go with you tomorrow,” she said.

Ragna looked up from the last note. “To the counting house?”

“To Blackwater.”

“I haven't said I was going.”

“No. You haven't.”

Neri was wrapping a tool roll. She had added a narrow rule, a small hammer and a pair of gloves patched at the fingers.

“We have a latch to fit at the works,” she said. “They ordered it last month. If we arrive to ask questions, somebody will decide we need permission. If we arrive to finish their door, somebody may let us finish it.”

“I will need the delivery records,” Ragna said.

“Then bring something to write on.”

Sella picked up the two pieces of paper, the unsigned receipt and the ledger fragment, and folded them inside her cash book.

“I'll ask Brann to come tonight. He carried Dorrik. He can tell me who told him to.”

Ragna put her own copy in the leather pouch that had held her desk key that morning. The key was still there. It seemed an absurdly small instrument for everything she had thought it protected.

At the threshold the warning bell gave its low note again. Orren, back under the bench, reached out with his file.

“It's her own coat,” he told the bell. “Let go.”
