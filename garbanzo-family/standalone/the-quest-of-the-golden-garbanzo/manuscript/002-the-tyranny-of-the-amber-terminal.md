# Chapter 2: The Tyranny of the Amber Terminal

By eight thirty, Jorge was twenty stories inside the Amber Terminal Citadel, watching an Event-Stream lose its manners across three glowing walls of Manhattan glass.

Outside, crisp November light bounced off the Hudson River like polished tin. Inside, thousands of golden numbers poured down glowing obsidian pipes, carrying trades, reports, and schedules across the empire. Somewhere near Gate Seven, an unchained Report-Rendering Dragon had sneezed mid-flight, scattering half-baked PDF wings into the air while an apprentice engineer stared in horror.

"Catch the queue before it splits the schema!" Jorge called out, slinging his satchel beside a desk console.

He snapped open his brass toolbox, grabbed two tuning forks and a copper patch-cable, and dove under the amber stream. Unlike the kitchen stove, where an egg could defeat him in forty seconds, pipelines made glorious sense. Messages needed clean handoffs, honest timestamps, and zero duplicate echoes. Two deft flicks redirected the choking torrent into a calmer side channel. A third command smoothed the dragon's ruffled tail feathers until every report page clicked together neatly in midair.

The apprentice let out a shaky breath. "Sir, how did you even see the bottleneck before the alarms rang?"

"Years of stubbornness," Jorge said, wiping glowing orange dust from his sleeve. "Plus a miniature brass chickpea back home that breaks far worse things before breakfast."

Before the boy could ask what on earth a brass chickpea had to do with platform architecture, the far corner of the hall grew chilly. Thin silver threads drifted down from the ceiling vents, silent as snow and twice as polite. A sleek machine with forty articulated arms glided past their row, weaving gleaming code tapestries in three seconds flat without once pausing for coffee.

The Automaton Loom.

Half the floor went quiet as people watched its silver needles flash. Nobody shouted. Nobody needed to. Every engineer in the Citadel felt the cold arithmetic behind those humming arms: the Loom never got tired, never asked about school recitals, and never hurried home to New Jersey before bedtime.

Jorge packed his tuning forks slower than usual. Years ago, climbing inside this Citadel had felt like conquering a crystal mountain. He had worked hard, endured team shifts, learned whole dialects of asynchronous dragons, and earned real craft. Still, watching the Loom spin silently overhead sharpened a question he had been dodging for months. If the tower kept shrinking the value of human hands, why spend his family's best years guarding someone else's pipes?

His evening train rattled back under the Hudson through dark tunnels that smelled of wet stone and warm pretzels. Halfway across the river, a reflection shimmered beside him in the black train window. It wore a tailored charcoal coat and smiled with velvet assurance.

The Someday Siren leaned close against the glass, her voice warm as hot cider.

"Look how well you handled Gate Seven today, Jorge," the Siren murmured above the clack of rails. "Stick around one more spring. Wait for one more March bonus. Wait until the Citadel turns completely gold, until every storm is impossible, until nobody could ever call you reckless."

Jorge rested his forehead against the cool window. Part of him almost nodded. A bonus meant extra margin. One more year meant a thicker shield against bad winters. Waiting felt wise, responsible, and grown-up.

"Wait until Clara reaches seventh grade," the Siren hummed softly, fading as the train burst out into New Jersey night. "There is always next summer."

The house smelled of warm garlic, roasted peppers, and cinnamon when Jorge unlocked the front door at half past seven. Clara had already changed into her unicorn nightshirt, though a smudge of purple marker sat firmly across her left eyebrow.

"Papa!" she cried, tackling his knees. "Did you wrestle the dragon?"

"One small sneezy dragon," Jorge said, hoisting her onto one hip. "Mostly I dodged polite silver spiders."

Marissa appeared from the hallway with a raised eyebrow. "Dinner is warm. Do not ask what the microwave did; I stopped it in time."

After Clara brushed her teeth and Marissa slipped upstairs to read three real chapters of wizard adventure with big bold letters, Jorge walked into his tiny study to check the weather for tomorrow's commute.

Something stiff crunched against his lamp switch.

Taped squarely across the dark glass of his desk monitor hung a thick piece of poster paper. Clara had drawn three stick figures beneath an impossibly bright yellow sun. One wore pink boots and held a tiny silver cat beside a stone fence. One carried a shiny golden compass. And the third figure, labeled *PAPA* in triumphant purple crayon, held a green padel racket in one hand and a coil of bright orange copper wire in the other.

Nowhere on the entire sheet was there a single glowing amber box.

Below the grass, in careful six-year-old letters that wobbled slightly near the margin, Clara had written six words:

*WHEN DO WE GET YOUR TUESDAYS?*

Jorge sat down in his office chair, still wearing his train coat, and stared at the drawing until his coffee went cold. Then he reached into his satchel, took out a red marker, and circled **SATURDAY** on the wall calendar twice.
