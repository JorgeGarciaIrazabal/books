# Chapter Fifteen: The Lower Gate

Ragna put the repaired pin through the brake arm and waited for Neri to secure it.

“Can it move?” she asked.

“Yes. Slowly. The rim wants work when Mere has it back.”

Kelm took a folded sheet from his jacket. He held it for a moment before giving it to Ragna. The folds were soft, the paper stained where it had rubbed against his shirt.

“Carrier's counterfoil,” he said. “It goes back with the vehicle. I am not taking their book.”

Ragna opened it. Forty bars, received at Thorn. The order reference matched the supplier's invoice. Beside the quantity were a stores number and Dole's signature. The batch mark was written out below, with a correction initialled by the receiver.

“Did you see them counted here?”

“Yes.”

“All forty?”

“I counted with him. That's why he put the wrong mark right. I told him it was wrong.”

She read the correction again. A receiving count, a named receiver and a batch that could be checked: evidence the bars had arrived, not merely been paid for. She put the counterfoil inside the collection authority so the old folds would not tear.

“It doesn't say anything about the twenty,” Kelm said.

“I know.”

“You keep asking about them.”

“We will have to ask someone else.”

He rubbed the empty place inside his jacket. She could see how exposed he felt without the paper, though it had done nothing to keep him safe here.

“If you want it back, say so,” she said.

“Give it to Mere. She owns the cart.”

Neri gathered her tools. She counted them once, stopped at the spare pin she had used, and took the bent one in its place. Then she rolled the cloth and tied it.

“We can ask Dole together,” Ragna said to Kelm. “We can tell him you have asked to leave and we will say where you are going. I won't tell you he will let you.”

“What if he doesn't?”

“I don't know yet.”

Neri lifted the sack onto the cart.

“Did you take the rail out?” she asked.

Kelm looked up sharply.

“Yes.”

“Before the load moved?”

“Yes.”

Ragna saw Neri's fingers close on the sack. She did not loosen them.

“My father knew?”

“He told me not to run it. I was told to keep it running.”

“By whom?”

Kelm glanced towards the office. “Harrow.”

A cart rattled in at the main gate. The sound came between them, hard wheels over stone. Ragna kept her eyes on Neri.

“Does that change what you want?” she asked her quietly.

Neri looked as if she might strike him. She had the hammer near her hand and did not pick it up.

“I want him outside,” she said. “I want to hear the rest where he can't keep looking at that door.”

Kelm swallowed. “If I don't say what you want?”

“You've said something I don't want already.”

“Neri,” Ragna said.

“No. I want him to understand. I am not forgiving him to get him through the gate.”

Ragna turned to Kelm. “You can ask to leave without agreeing to a statement. We will still have questions.”

He nodded once. Neri kept her eyes on the brake.

They lifted the cart's shafts and brought it out of the shed. Ragna and Neri took the front; Kelm steadied the rear crosspiece. The repaired brake caught when Neri pressed it. She tested it twice on the slope, then turned towards the office.

Dole came out with the return book.

“Finished?”

“Safe to move,” Neri said. “Rim damaged. Bracket straightened, pin replaced. Write that.”

He wrote it, holding the book against the wall. Ragna signed for the vehicle in that condition. Dole tore out the return receipt and gave it to her.

Kelm stepped beside the shafts.

“I'm leaving with them.”

Dole stared at him, then shut the book.

“You are required here.”

“I have asked to go. I have a room and work at Blackwater. If there are questions, tell me when to come.”

“Mr Harrow has not concluded his examination.”

“Then he can conclude it with me living somewhere else.”

Dole glanced at the guard by the main gate. He had not called him yet.

“Don't make this difficult.”

Kelm let go of the cart. “I have been sleeping in a shed.”

“You were given quarters.”

“A shed. With a bolt on the outside.”

Ragna set down her shaft. “Has he been detained under an order?”

“He is assisting an inquiry.”

“He no longer agrees to sleep here.”

“Nor does he agree to what happened at the lift, apparently.”

Neri took a breath so sharply that Ragna heard it. Dole looked at her, then away.

“I didn't mean that.”

“What did you mean?”

“I mean there is an account to be settled. I cannot let people come and go while the investigator is asking for them.”

“Ask him now,” Ragna said. “We can wait while you ask.”

Dole hesitated, then called the porter. He told him to hold the cart and went inside. Through the office window Ragna saw him kneel at the hearth. Green light rose against the wall.

Kelm took a step backwards.

“He'll tell him to keep me.”

“He hasn't answered yet,” Ragna said.

“You heard him at the shaft?”

“No.”

“I did.”

The porter stood beside them with his keys in his hand. Neri asked him whether he had an order to keep Kelm.

“I work the stores,” he said.

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I've got.”

From the lower lane came the creak of a loaded wagon. The river gate opened. A goblin in a wide-brimmed hat led his horse up the slope, pulling a load of charcoal. Neri recognised him at the same moment Ragna did. He was the carrier from the checkpoint, the one whose sack they had helped refill.

The porter waved him towards the storehouse. The lower gate remained open while another man brought the first barrow out to unload.

Kelm picked up the rear crosspiece.

“I am going,” he said.

Ragna looked at Neri. They had agreed on no signal, no way to do this safely. Neri turned the shafts away from the office and faced down the slope.

“Then help with the brake,” she told Kelm.

The porter stepped in front of the wheel.

“Don't do this.”

“I have signed for the cart,” Ragna said. “We are taking it out.”

“There are three of you.”

“Yes.”

He looked from her to Kelm. For a moment she thought he would reach for the wheel. Instead he took his hand off the wood and called Dole's name.

They moved slowly. The cart was light, but the slope pulled at it. Neri kept one hand on the brake arm. Ragna pulled to the left so they would not strike the charcoal wagon. Kelm leaned his weight against the rear crosspiece, his boots slipping once on the stones.

The guard shouted from the main gate.

“Stop there.”

Ragna looked back. His wand was out. The office door opened behind him.

“Stop the vehicle,” Dole called.

Something struck the right wheel. The shaft jerked out of Ragna's hand. The cart slewed sideways, and its crosspiece hit Neri above the ankle. She went down on one knee with a sound Ragna had never heard her make.

Kelm caught the brake arm. The repaired pin held. The cart stopped across the slope, one wheel lifted from the ground.

“Leave it,” Ragna said.

Neri reached towards her tool sack.

“Leave it.”

The charcoal horse threw its head up at the noise. Its driver pulled the reins and swore. The wagon moved broadside across the lane. The barrow man dropped his handles and went to the animal's head.

Dole shouted, “Not at the horse. Put it down.”

Ragna did not know whether he meant the wand or the vehicle. She had one hand beneath Neri's arm. Kelm took the other. Neri tried to stand, set weight on the injured foot and folded again.

“My tools,” she said.

“They're on the cart.”

“I know where they are.”

Ragna could reach the sack if she let go. She looked at the wheel hanging over the slope, the guard moving towards them, the narrow gap behind the charcoal wagon.

“We have to move.”

Neri shut her eyes. When she opened them, she nodded.

They took her between them, one arm over each shoulder. Behind the wagon, the driver looked down from his seat. He saw Ragna's face, Neri's foot dragging, Kelm's grey head.

“Hold the horse,” he told the barrow man.

“I am.”

“Then hold him another moment.”

He kept the reins tight. The wagon stayed across the lane. There was room behind it for people, not another vehicle.

Ragna and Kelm brought Neri through the gap and down towards the river. The gate porter took a step into their path.

“Collection chit.”

Ragna pulled the folded return receipt from her pocket and thrust it towards him. She did not stop long enough to see whether he took it. The sheet fluttered, caught on his coat and fell.

“We've got to get her home,” she said.

“There's another man.”

“I am going home,” Kelm said.

The porter caught his sleeve. Kelm twisted free. The cloth tore from shoulder to elbow. For an instant all three lost their balance. Neri cried out and gripped Ragna's collar. They stumbled through the open gate onto the towpath.

A man leading a packhorse stopped in front of them. He was a Muggle; Ragna knew it by his uncomprehending stare at the Ministry badge and the wand lifted behind the bars.

“What's happened?” he asked.

“She's hurt,” Ragna said. “Give us room.”

He drew his horse aside. From within the depot Dole called to the guard again. Ragna heard the words people on the path. No spell followed them across the open ground.

Ragna kept looking back.

They turned beneath the old mill wall, where the path bent out of sight of the gate. Kelm's breath came harshly. He had eaten badly and worked too long; his strength was not what the breadth of his shoulders promised. Neri tried to hop, then swore when the movement jarred her leg.

“Put me down.”

“Not here,” Ragna said.

“There are steps ahead.”

Neri was right. A short flight rose from the towpath towards the wooded bank. Ragna had not seen it. The first steps were worn hollow and slippery with moss.

They stopped long enough to change their grip. Kelm went behind Neri, taking most of her weight. Ragna went in front, holding her hands while she pushed herself up one step at a time. Halfway, Neri began to shake. She was furious about it and could do nothing to stop.

At the top lay the kiln path they had taken yesterday. Ragna knew the broken wall, the bed of nettles and the turn behind the coppice. She looked back. Nobody was visible on the lower steps. A voice called from the river, too distant to distinguish.

“Keep going,” Neri said.

They went into the trees.

The ruined kiln gave them cover from the path. Its round wall rose shoulder-high on the sheltered side. Kelm lowered Neri onto dry leaves and sat down so suddenly that Ragna thought his legs had failed.

“Only a minute,” he said.

Neri pulled her coat aside to look at the ankle. It was already swelling. Ragna knelt beside her, afraid to touch it.

“We need the healer.”

“And Mere,” Neri said.

Ragna looked at her.

“Her cart. We left it.”

“I know.”

“My tools.”

“I know.”

The collection authority had torn at one corner. The carrier's counterfoil was still inside it, protected by the folded oilcloth in Ragna's pouch. She took it out just long enough to see that Dole's signature and the forty-bar entry were dry.

Kelm watched her. “You got it?”

“Yes.”

He leaned his head against the old kiln wall.

Ragna wanted to ask him about the rail, about the order, about where Dorrik had stood. She looked instead at his split sleeve and Neri's hand clenched around a handful of leaves.

She put the papers away and began working out who could reach the road to ask for help without leaving the others unprotected.

“I can go,” Kelm said.

“You need to sit.”

“So do you.”

Ragna looked at the path beyond the broken wall. If he went alone, he could keep going. He knew where they were, and they had no way of making him return.

“There's a carrier's turn below the kiln,” Neri said. “Not the river path. Tell them we need a cart that takes a passenger lying down.”

Kelm got to his feet. “Which healer?”

Ragna gave him the name and address written on Fenn's bill. She had copied it before they left because she had meant to ask after him on the way home. She tore the little slip from her notebook and held it out.

He took it.

“Tell Sella where we are,” Neri said.

Kelm nodded. He waited until Ragna moved aside, then went through the gap in the wall. She listened to his footsteps until they faded into the ordinary sounds of the wood.
