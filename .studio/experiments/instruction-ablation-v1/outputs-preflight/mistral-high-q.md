## F1-D
The ticket room smelled of lamp oil and the iron stove ticking in the corner. Neri kept her right hand in her coat pocket, thumb hooked over the token’s edge, feeling its blank face through the wool. Beside her, Olin leaned on the counter with the easy sprawl of a man who had never once queued for anything.

“You could just ask,” she said.

“Ask what?”

“Why. The far bank has a name on it tonight, or it doesn’t.” She would not look at him. The window glass held two reflections, hers pale, his looming, and she watched the gap between them like a measurement she was afraid to finish.

Olin laughed, dry. “One trip. One. You always were good at dividing things evenly.”

“Then take your half and walk.” The words came out flatter than she meant. Underneath sat the old plea, the girl who had once traded her lunch for his good opinion. She hated that the wanting still lived in her chest, warm and stupid and loyal.

He reached for the pocket without asking. She stepped back against the door frame; the token stayed where it was. The lantern by the ticket window swung in the draft, and its light crossed his face and left it half in shadow.

“I’m boarding,” she said. “The seventeen-forty. You can stand here and be furious, or you can—” She stopped. Explaining would cost more than the token. “I’m boarding,” she said again, and let the sentence carry the weight: her apprenticeship, the gate at 17:55, twelve minutes on the water, the brother left on this side of it.

Her hand stayed on the token. She had not given it away. That was the commitment, made in the cold room while the stove ticked: she would cross, and he would not, and she would not spend the walk to the landing buying back his opinion with a coin she could not replace.

The doorbell chimed. Olin did not move. Outside, the river fog lifted off the water in one slow breath.

## F2-D
The studio door opened at 12:00 and let in Leena, Pia, and the smell of airport coffee. Rafi did not look up from the booking sheet. The key lay on the desk beside it, where he’d left it, where it had been since he unlocked the booth.

“Twenty minutes,” Leena said. “That’s the whole argument, apparently.”

Pia stood just inside the doorway with a violin case that had seen better decades. She looked at the booth, then the hallway, then the floor: a complete tour of anywhere but here.

Rafi waited for her to ask for him. She asked for the room.

“We could have half an hour,” she said, as if wanting it might rearrange the building. “Pia’s due—” She stopped, smiled at her daughter, unhelpful.

“I know when she’s due,” Rafi said. “I’m also due. At 12:30. With a paid audition that needs fifteen clean minutes, which is what this room has, minus the part where it’s already 12:03.”

Pia shifted the case from one hand to the other and held it a little tighter.

Leena’s shoulder went up. “Then we’ll wait out something useful.”

Rafi picked up the key. It was warm from the desk lamp, absurdly warm, like it had opinions. He thought of being useful, of the old band, of the silence between them that neither had named. He thought of the rent.

“Ask me,” he said.

Leena looked at him. The hallway was quiet enough to hear the radiator.

“I’m asking,” Leena said. “I need you. Not the booking. You.”

Rafi put the key in her palm and closed her fingers around it. Pia set the case down and lifted the violin onto the doorframe where the light was, and reached for the door.

“The room’s yours,” Leena said. “That’s all I’m taking.”

Rafi stepped back into the hallway. The booth door clicked. At 12:05 the red light above it came on, and he counted fifteen minutes toward his own missed audition, and let the number go.
