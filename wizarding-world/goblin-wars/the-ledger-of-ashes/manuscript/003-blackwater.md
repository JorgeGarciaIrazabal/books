# Chapter Three: Blackwater

Neri arrived at the counting house before the lamps were lit. She carried the latch in a sack and wore her father's leather apron folded twice at the waist.

In the night Ragna had dreamed that the wage slips were leaves and somebody had opened a window. She had checked the bundle on her desk three times already and could not put it down.

“You're bringing those?” Neri asked.

“No.”

“Good. I don't want to carry you if you drop them in the river.”

Ragna locked the drawer. Tavin was already at the back table, sorting withdrawal slips into piles. He had brought bread and a jar of pickled cabbage for his breakfast. The smell occupied the room.

“Work from the signatures,” Ragna told him. “If the amounts differ, set them aside. Don't choose the one you like.”

“I know.”

“I know you know.”

He looked at Neri's sack. “Would you like me to come?”

“No,” Ragna said. “I would like you to stay where somebody knows how much we owe.”

That pleased him for a moment. Then he remembered it was a reason to leave him behind.

They took a carrier's cart along the river road. Neri paid the fare before Ragna could find her purse and sat with the latch between her boots. Dawn showed the colour of the fields and very little else. At the edge of a wood, the carrier touched a knot tied into his reins. The horse turned down a lane Ragna would have sworn ended at a hedge.

Blackwater stood beyond it: a low yard, a row of brick sheds and a head frame rising above the trees. Steam escaped from a pipe near the ground. Somewhere underneath them a great weight moved, stopped and moved again.

The carrier unloaded two sacks of coke. Neri slid off the cart and took her sack to the gate.

A goblin in a patched coat came out of the gatehouse. One of his ears was folded at the tip.

“Repairs,” Neri said.

“We sent for your father.”

“Yes.”

The man looked past her, first at Ragna, then at the cart. “They've stopped visitors.”

“We're not visiting.”

He rubbed his hands together. Ragna could see the calculation: let them in and be blamed, keep them out and spend another day taking deliveries through a gate that would not stay shut. Neri waited without helping him.

“Fit it and go,” he said at last.

The latch belonged on a side door used by the handcarts. It had been held closed with rope. Neri cut the rope and set to work removing the old screws. Ragna stood with the tool roll, feeling an urgency that was of no use to either of them.

“You can look after those,” Neri said, giving her the screws.

“Where are the accounts kept?”

“Usually somewhere dry.”

“Neri.”

The young woman put down her hammer. “If they throw us out before this is fitted, they'll say we didn't finish and refuse to pay. Give me ten minutes.”

Ragna gave her ten minutes. Men passed them carrying tools or empty buckets. Nobody stopped. Several looked at Neri, looked again, and moved on without speaking. In a shed opposite, a pair of goblins were testing a length of chain, link by link. Each time they struck it, a small red light ran into the metal and faded.

One of them said something Ragna could not hear. The other laughed. Neri's hand slipped on the screwdriver.

“It's all right,” she said, though nobody had asked.

Ragna crouched and held the latch steady. Together they tightened the last two screws.

Neri tested it six times. On the seventh, she pressed a finger to the metal, and the tongue drew back before she touched the handle.

“It will need their keeper's hand,” she said. “I can leave it open until he comes.”

The gatekeeper accepted the new latch without praising it. He signed the repair bill on top of the gatepost.

“Where's the tally office?” Ragna asked him.

He looked at the bill as if it might answer for him. “Why?”

“I keep the workshop accounts. There's a delivery note I need to compare with yours.”

“What workshop?”

Neri gave the family name from the brass plate. His gaze dropped to her apron. At last he understood who she was.

“Past the boiler shed. Second door.” He lowered his voice. “Don't ask the wizard. Ask Hessa.”

The tally office contained a narrow desk and a stove too large for it. Hessa was a thickset goblin woman with a pencil tucked into her collar. She stood when she saw Neri, then sat again.

“You should have sent word,” she said.

“We brought the latch.”

“How's your mother?”

“Working.”

Hessa nodded as if it were the only possible answer. She shifted a pile of dispatch cards away from the wet patch beneath the window.

Ragna introduced herself and described the disputed delivery. She did not mention the concealed page.

“Forty bars on the twelfth,” Hessa said. “I remember. He came back about it.”

“When?”

“Day before yesterday.”

“Did you find the other twenty?” Neri asked.

Hessa put her hands under the desk. “Not here.”

“Where, then?”

“I don't know where.”

Ragna took out her paper. “What do your records show?”

Hessa looked towards the window. A wizard in a brown coat was crossing the yard with a roll of parchment under his arm.

“My records show forty,” she said.

“Sent to us?”

“Released for delivery.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No,” Hessa said. “It isn't.”

She pulled a wooden tray towards her. Small cards stood upright in it, divided by pieces of scrap tin. She found the twelfth and laid it on the desk. Two entries shared a destination. Each was for twenty bars. Dorrik's workshop was named at the top, but the second entry had a line across it and two letters written in the margin. The ink was the same shade as the original figures. Ragna could not tell which had been made first.

“May I copy it?”

“Names and quantities. Not the marks.”

“Why not?”

“Because I know how many people have seen that card, and so do they.”

Ragna put down her pen.

“If this is used, someone will ask you about it.”

“Someone is already asking.”

For the first time Hessa looked angry. She pushed the card back into its place.

“You may write that there were two loads. You saw that. You may write that I showed you. Don't write down a guess about where the second went and call it my words.”

“I won't.”

Neri had her arms folded tightly. “My father signed for both.”

“Your father signed a release at the top. The drivers signed their loads out at the gate. If a man takes a receipt to the bank, it does not mean he drove both carts.”

“Who drove the second?”

Hessa shook her head. Whether she did not know or would not say, Ragna could not tell.

Outside, the brown-coated wizard stopped a worker. He unrolled his parchment. The worker pointed towards the shaft.

Hessa closed the tray.

“I have to work.”

Ragna wrote two sentences while Hessa watched. She read them aloud. Hessa changed “delivered” to “released” and made her cross out “to the household” in the second sentence.

Then she said, “I never saw him come up.”

Nobody answered.

“People keep saying I did,” she said. “They see an office, they think we see everything from it.”

Ragna put the paper away. “Thank you.”

“Don't thank me yet.”

Brann was at the boiler shed, dragging a length of hose into a coil. He straightened when Neri approached. In daylight he looked younger than he had behind the handcart, with pale lines running through the soot at his throat.

“I went to your house,” he said.

“My mother said.”

“She asked me to come back tonight.”

“Will you?”

He worked one boot beneath the hose to stop it springing free. “If they let me finish.”

Ragna waited until two workers had passed. “I saw you yesterday morning.”

“I remember.”

“You told me he fell.”

Brann kept his eyes on the hose. “He did.”

“Were you there?”

“At the bottom.”

Neri made a small sound. Brann stopped coiling.

“I was at the bottom,” he repeated. “The lift came down wrong. One corner caught. I went around to see what was holding it, and he was there.”

“Was he alive?” Neri asked.

Brann's mouth moved before he spoke. “No.”

She turned away. Ragna let her go a few paces. There was a stack of empty crates by the wall; Neri put her hand on one and stood looking at the grain of the wood.

“Where did he fall from?” Ragna asked.

Brann glanced at the yard. “You should leave before Pell sees you.”

“The wizard?”

“He's taking statements. He likes them to agree.”

“Show me the place.”

“No.”

Ragna stepped back.

“My sister works the chain test,” he said. “My rooms belong to the works. You can come and ask a question, and then you can go home. I cannot.”

Ragna looked at the chain shed. Which one was his sister? She had not thought to ask.

“What can I ask you?” she said.

He stared at her. Then he pointed at the hose beneath his boot.

“Give me that end.”

She helped him lift it over a hook on the wall. He reached past her, pushed the coil flat and said, so quietly that she barely heard, “Count the empty holes. Left of the lift.”

Neri had returned. Brann picked up a bucket and went through the boiler shed without looking at either of them.

The lift stood in a fenced enclosure at the far end of the yard. A platform hung over a black opening, suspended on four chains. There was no wall to keep them from looking. A rope across the entrance carried a board: NO ADMITTANCE DURING EXAMINATION.

Neri stopped well short of it.

“Don't cross,” Ragna said.

“I wasn't going to.”

The platform was broad enough for a loaded cart. On either side, rails guided its wheels. Along the left edge of the shaft, a wooden barrier ran from a post near them to a second post beyond the opening. Another length should have met it at a right angle. Instead there were two empty sockets in the paving and four bolt holes in the adjacent upright. A beam lay against a wall ten feet away.

Neri crouched on their side of the rope.

“The corner rail,” she said.

“Can you tell when it was moved?”

“From here? No.”

She shifted to see past the platform. A dark scrape ran across the paving towards the edge. The stone around one socket was lighter than the rest.

“It hasn't been exposed to much dirt,” she said. “But it could have been under a cloth. Or scraped clean. I can tell you someone took it apart with tools.”

“Could a load have knocked it down?”

“Not and taken the bolts out whole.”

The bolts lay in a shallow tin beside the beam. Ragna counted four. She wrote the count, the sockets and the position of the beam. She drew the platform badly, then asked Neri to correct it.

“You cannot be here.”

Pell stood behind them. He had a square face, a narrow strip of beard and a wand in his hand. He did not raise it. He did not need to.

“We haven't crossed the rope,” Ragna said.

“The works are under examination.”

“We fitted a latch.”

“Then you may leave.”

Neri held up the signed bill. Pell took it, read it and gave it back. He looked at her apron.

“Your name?”

She told him.

“Ah,” he said.

Ragna disliked him for that more than she had expected.

“We need the delivery records,” she said. “A charge has been made against her household for materials they haven't received.”

“You may submit a request through the proper office.”

“Which office?”

“The bank will advise you.”

“Did you examine the rail before it was moved?”

He looked at her paper.

“Give me that.”

“It is my working note.”

“It concerns an examination in progress.”

“Then take a copy.”

He held out his hand. Behind him Brann appeared at the boiler shed door. Pell did not see him, but Ragna did. She could keep the paper for another ten seconds, perhaps. She could argue until Pell used his wand. Neither would keep Brann's whispered words off the page if he took it.

They were not on it. She had written only what she had seen.

She gave him the sheet.

He read it without moving his lips, folded it and put it in his pocket.

“The gate,” he said.

Neri gathered her tools. Ragna walked beside her, feeling the absence of the note more acutely than she had felt the weight of the whole ledger. At the gate, two men were fastening a notice to the post. Hessa stood outside her office with her arms at her sides. One of the chain testers swore and was told to lower his voice.

Pell called across the yard. “Until the material inquiry is concluded, no persons employed here may remove tools, records or stores without written authority.”

The gatekeeper looked at Neri's sack.

“Ours,” she said. “We brought them.”

He glanced at Pell, then opened the gate just wide enough for them to pass.

On the road, Neri stopped and unrolled her apron. Ragna thought she meant to take it off. Instead she held up the inside flap. In the dust on the leather she had traced four circles and two short lines.

“Four bolts,” she said. “Two sockets.”

“Yes.”

“Left of the lift. Beam against the wall.”

“Yes.”

Neri folded it inward carefully.

“You have paper?”

Ragna took out the last piece Sella had cut for her. They sat on a fallen trunk. Ragna redrew the platform while Neri watched each stroke.

“We have to get Brann out,” she said.

“He has a sister.”

“Then both.”

Ragna put the pen cap on. Beyond the hedge, a hammer began striking the gate as the men fixed the last corner of the notice. She could not see the yard now, or the people in it.

“We will have to ask them,” she said.
