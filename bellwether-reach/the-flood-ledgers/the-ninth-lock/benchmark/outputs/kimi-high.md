## P3

The Registry lamp had burned low enough to flatter the seals. Sera kept her splinted wrist inside her coat and her eyes on the blotter.

"You're early," Ivo said.

"The tide isn't."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was half of what had made him unbearable and all of what had made him useful. "The chair creaks," he said. "I oiled it anyway. In case."

"I didn't come for the chair."

"No." He turned the inkwell a quarter inch, squaring it to the desk's edge. "You came the way you left. Straight line. No looking at the walls. I used to time it on the slate — how long before you'd stop looking at the walls."

Her name was somewhere in this room with his line through it. Neatly. "What is on the walls, Ivo?"

"Nothing you asked about." He folded his hands, the apprentice's habit, waiting for her to check his sums. "You never asked what it cost, being the one who stayed. You asked whether I'd dropped the seal."

"You dropped it."

"I caught it," he said. "That's the part you didn't stay for."

The kettle ticked somewhere behind the stacks. Sera thought of the North certificate against her ribs and the second one she could not see. He would not hand it over for courtesy. He might hand it over for a debt, and she would not offer him one.

So she sat down. The chair did not creak.

Ivo looked at her the way he used to look at a column of figures before telling her where the error was. Then he reached past the inkwell and dragged the blotter toward her an inch — no more. An invitation with conditions.

"South's paper," he said. "You can read it here."

Not custody. Not yet. But the first thing he had given her freely in two years, and they both knew it counted.

## P4

The Registry kept its own weather. Under the door a wet seam shone where the rain had come in and been permitted to stay. Sera moved a chair out of it with her boot and did not sit.

"Five o'clock," Ivo said from behind the desk, as if the hour needed a clerk.

"North's certificate," she said, and put it on the blotter. The seal winked copper in the lamplight. "Read me the other one."

"There is no other one on the docket."

"I didn't ask the docket."

He had aged somewhere she wasn't, which was most places now. He squared the inkwell to the desk's edge with two fingers — the old habit, checking the world for errors and finding them in the furniture. "Registry originals don't leave this room, and they don't get read across my desk because a lock inspector asks nicely. You taught me that. The rule was yours."

"The rule covered one certificate at a time."

Silence. Not empty — inventorying. Then Ivo opened the drawer, took out a folded paper, and laid it beside hers without touching either. South Basin's name. The same tide date. The same seal, struck true, the Registry's mark biting clean through the wax.

She had the loupe out before she decided to. Right hand only; the left sat in its splint like a parcel addressed to someone else. One seal. Two seals. Both authentic. Anyone could buy wax. Nobody bought the Registry's bite but the Registry.

"Both registered," she said. "Both fee-paid. One lock."

"Yes."

"Say the rest."

"The lock can't read." His voice stayed level, clerical. "It doesn't know it's been promised twice. It protects what's assigned, and North is assigned. You know the arithmetic better than I do."

She did. Bell at half past. Forty minutes to change an assignment once the bell had rung, twenty minutes to walk there, tide at six. Whoever wanted South drowned only had to keep this room's mouth shut until dark, and the law would call the drowning correct.

"Freeze it," Ivo said. "That's the honest move. Your license on the desk, a hearing in a fortnight, and North stays protected in the meantime, which is what it says on that paper." He did not push anything at her. He didn't have to. He had priced the honest move like a fee.

A freeze would seal the lie in wax and call it pending. South would drown correctly, in the proper order, with her name somewhere in the file.

"No," she said.

She folded South's certificate once — her right hand doing everything now, thumb and knuckles — and slid it inside her coat against the North one. Then she took his pen and wrote in his daybook: *Requisitioned by Venn, both originals, inspection authority.* She had no such authority over Registry originals, and the daybook knew it, and Ivo, watching her write, knew it best of all.

"Sera." Her name, not her title. First time in two years. "If you walk out with those, I can't say you didn't."

"I know."

"They'll take the license anyway. Without the hearing. Without the tidy part."

"Then it's mine they take, and not in my absence."

He stood very still. Whatever he had wanted from her tonight — she could see it standing behind his face like weather behind glass — he did not get it. He went to the door instead and opened it, and looked at the corridor, not at her.

"Twenty minutes to the lock," he said. "Nineteen now."

The brass key turned in her right pocket under her thumb, the one warm thing on her. Sera left the chair standing in the dry and went out into the rain.

## P8

One lock. Two basins. One tide.

Sera Venn inspects the Ninth Lock, the gate that decides each high tide which of the Reach's two basins stays dry. Tonight the Registry has done the impossible on paper: two certificates, two authentic seals, one protection, legally owed to both. North holds the assignment and her brother's fields. South holds the seed store every basin plants from. The proof sits in a drawer kept by Ivo Renn, the apprentice who once ruled a neat line through her name, and the only lawful remedy costs her license and settles nothing before six o'clock.

The bell rings at half past five. The tide does not attend hearings. Somewhere in the Registry, two papers were made true — and saving something this evening means choosing what may be lost.
