# Chapter Six: The Dispatch Clerk

The bank accepted its petition at nine. The Ministry accepted its petition at ten, after Ragna had corrected the heading twice and paid for a copy she had not asked to have made.

The receiving clerk pressed a seal into the receipt.

“When will it be answered?” she asked.

“When it has been considered.”

“By whom?”

“The appropriate officer.”

“Put the officer's name on the receipt.”

He looked at her over the top of his spectacles. Behind her, Fenn shifted the bundle of papers from one arm to the other. They had come together because the meeting had agreed that no one should carry both petitions alone.

“I cannot put a name down before the referral,” the clerk said.

“Then write that.”

He sighed, but wrote it. Ragna waited until the ink was dry before she accepted the receipt.

Outside, Fenn bought a hot pie from a woman whose stall stood on the boundary between the visible street and the little court where the Ministry kept its provincial office. When a Muggle cart passed, the court showed only a disused archway. The stallholder fed the archway a scrap of pie crust as casually as she fed the birds.

“For the rats,” she told Fenn when he stared.

“I wasn't asking.”

“They're the only ones who pay attention to the sign.”

Fenn divided the pie with his knife. The filling was mostly potato. Ragna took her half and ate while he held the receipts, the first useful thing they had received that morning.

Ilva was waiting at the counting house.

She had taken the chair facing the door, not the one beside Ragna's desk. The blackened mark on her cuff had been cut away and the edge turned under with small, uneven stitches.

“I need to speak to you,” she said.

“You can.”

“Elsewhere.”

Ragna put her bag down. “These are working hours.”

“I know what hours they are.”

Tavin watched them from the table. Ragna had given him the Ministry receipt. He read the sentence about the officer whose name was not yet known, then turned the paper over as if there might be a better answer on the back.

Ilva lowered her voice. “There is someone you should meet.”

“Who?”

“A Ministry dispatch clerk.”

“What does he want?”

“To be certain he is not the only person left with his name on an instruction.”

Ragna took off her coat slowly. She could still feel the strongroom key passing between them, the dry strip hidden beneath it. She had been waiting for Ilva to explain that moment. Now she did not trust the relief it offered her.

“Why did you give me the fragment?”

Ilva glanced at Tavin.

“He was there,” Ragna said.

“I saw you open the page. If Harrow had found the strip, he would have asked why you had it.”

“So you took it and gave it back where he couldn't see.”

Ilva pressed her hands together. “I had already done enough.”

“What had you done?”

“The page was covered at my direction.”

Tavin put down the receipt.

Ragna had suspected it. Now Ilva sat in front of her admitting she had ordered it done.

“What was on it?”

“A reconciliation.”

“Between what?”

“Accounts that should not have been combined.”

Ragna waited. Ilva looked at the door, then at the open wage books.

“I will tell you what I can prove. Not here.”

“Dorrik's family hears it too.”

“I did not come to arrange a meeting.”

“Then you came to the wrong person.”

Ilva stood. For a moment Ragna thought she would leave. Instead she picked up her coat.

“His name is Daniel Venn. He has asked me not to bring a crowd.”

“Two people is not a crowd.”

“Sometimes it is.”

Ragna went to Sella's workshop and put the choice to her. Neri was shaping the last of the school fittings. The carrier had failed to collect them on the promised day; the school had sent word that he would come tomorrow instead. She listened without taking her hands from the vice.

“I'll stay,” she said. “We promised these for collection. But I want to know what he says.”

“You will,” Sella said.

“Not just what they think it means.”

“All of it.”

Sella put on her coat. She did not take the cash book containing the original fragment. Ragna did not ask where she left it.

Ilva led them to a street above the river where the houses leaned towards one another. At the end was a shop selling ink, sealing wax and paper in small quantities. The glass in its door had a crack repaired with strips of copper. Inside, a woman was cutting a sheet of paper into quarters while she argued with a customer about the price of the offcuts.

Daniel Venn sat at a table behind the counter. He was a thin wizard with a sunken chin and cuffs polished by years of desk work. A wand lay beside his penknife. He covered both with a sheet when Sella looked at them.

“I don't work here,” he said. “My aunt owns the shop.”

The woman at the counter called, “He pays for what he uses.”

“I told you there would be two,” Ilva said.

“You said one and perhaps another.”

“Here is another,” Sella said.

Venn drew a chair out from under the table. It was too high for her. He began to apologise; she put her foot on the lower rung and climbed up before he finished. Ragna took the stool.

On the wall hung a Ministry calendar. Albert Boot's name appeared in elaborate lettering beneath an engraving of the Ministry seal. Someone had pinned a list of household purchases over the lower part of the page. Ink, onions, lamp oil, two yards of flannel. The flannel had been crossed out.

Venn followed her glance and stood in front of the calendar.

“What has Ilva told you?”

“That you have put your name on something.”

“I put it on hundreds of things.”

“Then start with the one that brought you here.”

He rubbed a thumb along the edge of the table. Under the thin skin of his wrist, a pulse beat visibly.

“There are dispatch instructions for materials leaving Blackwater. I copy them into the Ministry schedule. The works keeps the release card. The receiving depot signs its own counterfoil. Usually the three agree.”

“Usually?”

“If a load changes destination, the instruction is amended. It should be amended on each copy.”

Sella leaned forward. “Twenty bars meant for our workshop.”

Venn looked at Ilva.

“She knows about the two loads,” Ragna said. “She hasn't been told where the second went.”

“It went to Ministry stores.”

“Which stores?”

He put his hand inside his coat, stopped and took it out empty.

“What happens when I tell you?”

“We ask for the receiving record,” Ragna said.

“You won't be given it.”

“Then we will know who refused.”

“You talk as if they mind.”

Sella spoke before Ragna could answer. “What do you want us to do?”

Venn looked towards the counter. His aunt was wrapping the customer's paper in old newspaper, her fingers quick on the fold.

“My mother lives with me. She cannot manage the stairs without help. My sister comes twice a week. Neither of them works for the Ministry. Neither of them knows what I changed.”

“Did someone threaten them?” Ragna asked.

“Not in those words.”

“Which words?”

“He asked whether I still lived over the baker's. Said the steps would be difficult for my mother in winter. We have never discussed my mother.”

“Harrow?”

Venn nodded.

Sella put her hands on the edge of the table. “I cannot promise he won't come to your door.”

“I was told you had helped people leave the works.”

“Two people asked somewhere to sleep,” she said. “We found a room. It has a door. It isn't a fortress.”

“If I testify, they can take my post. They can say I altered the records.”

“Did you?”

He looked at her.

“Did you?” Sella asked again.

“Yes.”

The answer seemed to cost him more than the account of Harrow's visit. He sat back and pulled at one cuff.

“Then I won't say you didn't,” she said.

“I need protection.”

“We can ask about a room for your family. We can bring someone to hear your statement. We can't decide what happens to you for what you've done.”

Ilva had not spoken for some time. Now she said, “That applies to me as well.”

Venn turned on her. “You said it would be held inside the bank.”

“I was wrong.”

“What else were you wrong about?”

She took the question without looking away. “More than I have told them.”

Sella went still beside her. Ragna wanted to demand the rest from Ilva, but Venn was reaching into his coat. She let him.

He laid a folded sheet on the table.

“This is my dispatch duplicate. It records the altered destination and the instruction under which I made the change. The other twenty bars went to the Thorn depot. Stores number three.”

Ragna did not touch it until he pushed it towards her. The paper carried the Blackwater release date, the quantity twenty, and Sella's workshop name struck through with a single line. Below was a new destination, written in a different hand. At the bottom Venn had initialled it. Beside his initials was a reference to a separate order.

“Whose writing?” she asked.

“Mine. All of the alteration.”

“Was the instruction Harrow's?”

“Through his office. His name is on the order. It is not on this copy.”

Ragna read the distinction aloud, then wrote it on a separate sheet. Sella watched her pen.

“Did you know we hadn't agreed?” she asked.

Venn said nothing.

“Not when you received the instruction. When you copied it.”

“There should have been a notice to your workshop.”

“Was there?”

“I didn't send one.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“No. There wasn't.”

Outside, someone called to the baker across the street. Venn flinched. His aunt opened the door and told the caller he was at the wrong number. The door shut again.

“I kept this because there was another order,” Venn said. “A larger procurement. Forty bars, separately paid. I was told to enter both consignments under security stores and leave the source column blank on the public schedule.”

“Separately paid by whom?” Ragna asked.

“I don't have that voucher. I handle dispatches, not payment.”

She copied the second order number and boxed the reference. Forty-bar orders were common at the works; the quantity alone proved nothing.

“Is the duplicate yours to keep?” she asked.

“It's the Ministry's.”

“Then why do you have it?”

“Because when the inquiry began, the copies were called in. I said this one had been spoiled.”

“You lied.”

“Yes.”

He looked exhausted by the word. Sella turned the sheet over, examining the blank back, then laid it down again.

“Why now?”

Venn looked at Ilva. “She told me a man had died.”

“People had been waiting for wages before he died,” Sella said.

“I didn't know about the wages.”

“You knew whose iron it was.”

He did not deny it.

Ragna put the duplicate between them. “Will you sign what you've told us?”

“Where will it go?”

“To the inquiry, if it opens. To the people whose accounts are affected, if you agree. We won't add your family's address.”

“And if I don't?”

“We have seen the paper. We can say that. We can say what we saw written on it. Without you, we cannot prove who changed it or why.”

He took his wand from under the covering sheet. Ragna held still. He used its tip to light the little lamp at his elbow, then set it down and picked up his pen.

“Write the words as I say them,” he told her. “Not what you think I ought to have said.”

She did.

His statement took less than a page. He corrected a date and crossed out “ordered” in favour of “instructed in writing”. When it was finished, he signed each of the two sheets. Ilva signed beneath as witness. Sella signed after her.

Venn kept the dispatch duplicate. He let them make two copies and wrote across both that they had been compared with the paper in his possession. He did not agree to come to a public meeting. He did agree to speak again after his mother had decided whether to leave the rooms above the baker's.

At the door, his aunt put a small parcel in his hand.

“Flannel,” she said. “I had some in the back.”

He looked at her as if she had offered him something unbearable. She pushed him out of the way and served the next customer.

They walked back along the river with Ilva between them. Sella carried one signed copy. Ragna carried the other.

“You said more than you have told us,” Ragna said.

Ilva kept walking. “Yes.”

“Tomorrow morning. At the workshop. You explain the page you hid.”

“I have work.”

“So do we.”

Ilva stopped where the path narrowed around a tree root. She looked older in the open light. There were seams in her collar that someone had mended carefully, unlike the cuff.

“If I tell you, I cannot put it back.”

Sella moved past her. “We have a shovel at home with earth still on it. Think about what you can put back.”

Ragna waited until Ilva began to walk again. The copied dispatch in her pouch had lost the warmth of the shop. She kept one hand over it on the way home.
