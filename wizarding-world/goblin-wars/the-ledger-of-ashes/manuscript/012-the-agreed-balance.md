# Chapter Twelve: The Agreed Balance

Ragna read the first name twice and still said it wrong.

“Tesh,” Sella corrected. “Not Tersh.”

Ragna put a finger under the letters. For years the rope-maker had brought little coils to twist while he waited at the counting house. She had entered his name hundreds of times.

“Give me the other frame,” she said.

Neither the frame nor her lenses had changed. She had slept for an hour in Sella's chair, with her coat over her knees, and woken when Orren came in asking whether everyone was going to stay for breakfast.

Neri fetched the frame anyway. Ragna put it beside the first and did not use it.

Issa arrived at nine with Halrik and a bank clerk carrying a locked cash case. They had agreed to the workshop because Sella's supporting books were there, Fenn could not come to the bank house, and Ragna had put both reasons in writing. The clerk waited for a place to set the case. Neri cleared a tray of hinges.

Issa took off her gloves. She was a small goblin with a broad forehead and a habit of reading the signature on a page before she read its contents. Halrik was younger and wore the plain coat of someone who expected it to be damaged. He checked the leg of the rocking stool before sitting, then put a folded scrap of leather beneath it.

The letterhead they brought said THE WORKS BANK. Ragna had spent eleven years beneath the same name. The bank held the pooled reserves of the district's workshops and settled many of their metal purchases through its London accounts. Merrok managed those accounts. It had never made the deposited money his.

Issa laid out the terms of the examination.

“We can authorise advances with two reserve signatures. We will not certify a balance because a room wants us to. We will not wait for the iron inquiry before looking at wages that have nothing to do with it.”

“There are also wages that do,” Sella said.

“Then we look at what is disputed and what is not.”

Ragna opened the first packet. Tavin had put red thread around it. She untied the knot carefully, keeping the little loop intact.

They began with Tesh's rope workshop. His day tallies agreed with the wage book. Two withdrawals had matching signatures. A third had been entered twice in the household copy and once in the bank slip; he had already signed the correction. No disputed metal charge touched that account.

Halrik asked who had compared the bank slips.

“Arven,” Ragna said.

“Where are the originals?”

“At the counting house. In his custody.”

“And these?”

“Working copies, compared and signed in front of two clerks.”

Halrik sent the bank clerk to ask Arven to bring the originals. Ragna pressed her palms against the table.

“He signed them.”

“Yes,” Halrik said. “I want to see what he signed them against.”

She looked at the copy. Three days earlier she would have asked the same question. She put it down and let the clerk go.

Hedra came at half past nine. She had made no repair to the carrier's cover since Ragna left her the night before. She held the package receipt in a pocket and touched it when she sat down.

“Any word?” Ragna asked.

“No.”

“I haven't had any either.”

“Then don't stand up every time the door opens.”

Ragna sat. She had not known she was doing it.

Fenn came in a hired chair carried by two of the fitters, complaining that his legs worked. The healer had told him to keep the hand still and avoid jarring it. Sella gave the men money from the household purse, not the relief fund. Fenn saw it and began to object.

“Later,” she said.

His right wrist lay in a rigid support beneath his coat. He could not get his left sleeve off without help. Neri helped and said nothing about how carefully he had once checked a pane of glass for her.

Issa asked whether he needed to be there.

“Yes,” he said.

She moved the papers so he could read without leaning on the injured arm.

Arven arrived with the bank clerk and the original slips. He had put on his better coat. It made him look as if he expected to be accused of something requiring respectability.

The examination took the rest of the morning. Receipts agreed with tallies; names appeared where they should. Payments remembered but never written down went in the disputed column. Issa would not let Ragna treat memory as proof simply because she liked the person remembering. She also would not let Halrik treat an unsigned scrap as worthless when three independent books recorded the same payment.

At noon they had three balances they were willing to stand behind: Tesh's household, Fenn's workshop earnings, and Edda's furnace share. Edda's previous conditional payment had to be credited. She brought its receipt, angry at the sight of it but determined not to be paid twice for the same days.

“What about my aunt?” she asked.

“The registered allowance is not part of the wages we are examining,” Issa said. “I will look at that decision separately.”

“Today?”

“I cannot promise today.”

Edda sat back. She did not thank her for a promise she had not made.

Sella's account remained open. The school payment had reached her directly after the quarter closed; it did not settle what the bank already owed. Her household books supported the work and the withdrawals. The material deductions were still tied to the seized ledger.

“Pay what cannot be disputed,” she said.

Halrik drew her wage total towards him and wrote the price of forty bars beneath it. “If we set the second charge aside entirely, the first signed charge still leaves no positive balance on the figures we have verified.”

“Twenty bars reached me, not forty.”

“I have your stock book and the dispatch copy. The note your husband signed records a release, and the receiving record for the other load is missing. I am not prepared to decide where that load went on a copy alone.”

Sella took the pencil from him. She crossed out his forty and wrote twenty. Under that figure there was money to pay. She left the paper between them.

Halrik looked at the authority for the separate forty-bar purchase. “This may concern money moved from the reserve. It does not by itself show which household entry received the debit.”

“Ilva has signed that she checked it,” Ragna replied.

“We need to hear her.”

“She has been asked to come.”

“And the supplier's voucher?”

“Requested.”

He put the authority back. “Then this part is not finished.”

Sella's hand closed on the table edge. She let go before anyone asked her to.

Issa turned to the three agreed balances. The advances would cover the verified net earnings, less withdrawals and conditional relief already received. Gifts from the fund would not be deducted. Each receipt reserved every disputed claim against the bank and the Ministry.

Ragna read that sentence aloud. Edda made her read it again.

“The cash comes from the partners' working reserve,” Issa added. “Not from a fresh charge against another workshop. These three advances will be entered separately, with these receipts attached. Any further recovery is the bank's business. Nobody here is being paid to abandon a claim.”

Both partners signed. The bank clerk opened the cash case.

Tesh counted his money, then gave the clerk one coin back.

“One too many,” he said.

The clerk checked, reddened and corrected the count. Tesh put the rest into his coat and went to pay the people waiting at his workshop. He did not stay to hear what happened to anyone else's balance.

Edda took the amount remaining after her earlier payment. It was not enough to remedy the excluded allowance, but it was hers. She wrapped it in a cloth and tucked it inside the repaired lantern frame, which she had brought without the broken glass. Fenn could not sign with his right hand. He made a mark with his left, had it witnessed, and asked Sella to count the coins again before she put them in his purse.

He would have trusted his own fingers yesterday. Today he watched hers.

Hedra stayed in her chair. Her son's release was not among the things the cash case could provide.

Merrok arrived as the last receipt was being signed.

He stood in the doorway until Issa looked up. Behind him was Ilva, carrying her control book against her chest.

“I would have attended,” he said, “if I had known payments were to be made.”

“You knew an examination was offered,” Issa replied.

“An examination is not an instruction to advance cash on uncertified accounts.”

“Then you will be relieved that we examined them first.”

He did not take the chair Neri set out. He looked at the three receipts and at Fenn's supported wrist.

“I have lodged my concern about the violence surrounding these claims.”

“They are wages,” Fenn said. “My wrist hasn't changed which days I worked.”

“The bank must consider the circumstances.”

“Consider them after you've counted.”

Ragna watched Issa. The partner had put her gloves beside the signed instructions. She did not move them.

Merrok turned towards Ilva.

“The control book is bank property. It should be in the examiner's file.”

Ilva went past him to the table. “I have brought it for examination.”

“By whom?”

“Two reserve partners.”

She set it in front of Issa. For a moment her hand remained on the cover. Then she lifted it away.

The room had become very quiet. Even Orren, working on his rivets at the far bench, had stopped making small metal sounds.

Issa read the advance entry and the transfer. She looked at Ilva's tick, then at the copy Ragna held.

“You certified this?”

“Yes.”

“You will make a full account of what you certified and the instruction under which you did it.”

“I have made one.”

“I want it to the partners.”

Ilva took the folded statement from her case. Her fingers shook enough that she had to place the sheet flat before pushing it across.

Merrok said, “We should discuss this in the bank house.”

“The figures are here,” Issa replied.

“So are people without any duty of confidence towards the bank.”

Sella looked at the control book. “I seem to have contributed money without being trusted to see where it went.”

Halrik put a clean sheet over the unrelated entries. “The other accounts remain private. This one will be examined.”

Merrok sat at last. He had given up trying to stop the room and would now try to make a different use of it.

“The advance was made to secure a Ministry undertaking,” he said. “A temporary arrangement. It was never intended as a permanent charge on the contributing workshops.”

“Yet their balances were closed against it,” Issa said.

“They were not closed.”

“Their wages were stopped.”

“The record was seized.”

“With your signature,” Ragna said.

He looked at her. “After an order I could not refuse.”

“You signed before you asked us for totals.”

“Because I was trying to protect the rest of the bank.”

Hedra rose so abruptly that everyone turned.

“I'm going to ask at Blackwater again,” she said. “Anyone who wants to protect something may finish before I come back.”

Vessa went with her. Sella found food to put in the bag. For a minute all the elaborate accounts had to wait while Hedra checked that it was something Tavin would eat.

When they had gone, Merrok asked to speak to Ragna in the passage. She looked at Sella, who said, “We'll be here.”

Outside he stood well away from the door.

“I can have the disputed balance examined separately,” he said. “And your suspension reconsidered. You know the books. The bank still needs someone who knows them.”

“Then let me finish with the partners.”

“There is a simpler account. A temporary posting was carried forward when it should have been reversed. An examiner misunderstood an instruction. The loss can be remedied.”

“And the iron that never arrived?”

“Another account.”

“And Dorrik?”

“I did not put him at the shaft.”

“No. But you are asking me to stop where your responsibility stops.”

“I am asking you to help secure a payment. You have seen how slowly this proceeds when every correction becomes an accusation.”

Through the door came the scrape of Issa's pen. Ragna could smell the onions Sella had put in the soup. She wanted to sit down, to be told how much would be paid and when, to go back to an office where she could finish a task without somebody being taken away for it.

“Put the offer in writing,” she said.

“You know what I mean.” His voice had lost its force.

“Yes. Put it in writing.”

He left her in the passage.

Inside, Issa had taken custody of the control book for the partners' examination and given Ilva a signed receipt. Halrik was copying the procurement reference. Neither had authorised the disputed iron balance. Both had agreed that the Ministry advance required a separate accounting to the contributing workshops.

Ragna sat beside Sella. In the advance authority the supplier's name stood above the purchase reference. It had been there every time she read the paper. The bank held one bill, but the supplier would have kept another.

She wrote the name on a clean sheet and asked Neri where the road to the foundry left the river.
