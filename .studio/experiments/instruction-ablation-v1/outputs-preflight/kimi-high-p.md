## F1-D

The ticket room smelled of coal smoke and wet rope. The stove ticked like a clock that had lost interest in minutes. Olin stood with his back to the door — his old spot, between people and their leaving.

"Give it, Neri."

"Say why."

"I have to see someone."

"Someone." She kept her right hand in her coat pocket, thumb on the token's worn edge, the brass gone smooth from years of waiting. Six years he'd been gone. He'd come back taller, which she resented, and hollow-cheeked, which she resented more.

"The ferry will wait," he said.

"The ferry waits for the harbormaster, and the harbormaster's dead. Try another."

Something moved in his face and went under, like a fish turning in deep water. "Ask me anything else."

"That's not how questions work."

"That's how this one works."

The clock over the grille said five twenty-two. Eight minutes to the landing, and the boards iced at the steps. If she left now she'd make it. If she stood here she'd barely make it, and she'd never know why he'd come, and the not-knowing would sit in her like a swallowed pin.

"You could tell me a lie I'd like," she said. "You used to be good at those."

He looked at her then, the way he used to when she'd mended his net badly and he'd pretended the knots were fish-proof. She hated how long she'd been waiting for that look.

She took the token out of her right pocket. It held her thumb's warmth. She held it out between them.

"Don't tell me," she said. "If you tell me, I'll want to believe you, and I can't afford the wanting."

He reached. She let go. The token was in his hand now, and his hand closed over it, and the cold where it had been was hers to keep.

"Twelve minutes," she said. "Walk fast. Don't run on the steps."

When the door shut, she sat by the stove. Across the water, past the landing and the twelve-minute crossing, a gate had her name in chalk until five fifty-five. Chalk doesn't keep.

## F2-D

The booth key sat on the desk where Rafi had set it at eleven fifty-five, beside the striped sticky note that said DO NOT EAT THE GOOD SNACKS, an old label, no longer accurate.

Leena filled the doorway the way she used to fill second verses — like she'd been invited by physics. Pia stood half behind her, violin case held upright, studying the doorframe screws as though screws were the reason they'd come.

"Twelve oh-one," Leena said. "You're not in there."

"Observant. You should record an album of that."

"The hallway smells like pennies," Pia said, which was her review of the whole building.

"Fifteen minutes," Leena said. "Pia's tape. Due at half past. Same as yours." She said it the way you'd mention weather — nobody's fault, and certainly not hers.

Ask, he thought. Not for the room. For me.

The countdown clock on the desk ticked to twelve oh-two. Whoever started at twelve oh-five ran clean to twenty past; whoever didn't, didn't.

"There's the engineer's rate," Leena said. "I could pay it back."

"There's no engineer."

"Then I'll owe you the idea of him."

Pia's bow hand had gone white at the knuckles around the case handle. Rafi knew that knuckle. He'd shared a bathroom with it for nine years.

He took the key off the desk. Leena's chin lifted a quarter inch — victory, pre-thanked — and he went past her, crouched, and put the key into Pia's hand, folding her fingers over it like a lit match.

"Count yourself in," he said. "Red light means we're all very quiet."

Pia looked at the key, then at him, and nodded once, like a professional accepting terms. She stepped inside.

On his phone, a reminder named AUDITION in capital letters waited to become trivia. The clock said twelve oh-five. He watched Pia set the key on the stool, square her shoulders, and raise the bow, and he pressed the red button himself.
