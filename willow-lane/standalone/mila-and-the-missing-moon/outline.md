# Causal outline (planning, not accepted canon)

One afternoon on Willow Lane, from about two until dusk. The library opens into a small paved yard, beyond which a path leads to the community garden. A garden shed stores supplies. A lantern walk begins at the library after sunset. No open flames: lanterns use small battery lights.

Solution to plant fairly: the big, round moon was inside a shallow square cardboard box for protection. MOON was written on one side; an old TO THE GARDEN message was on the other. A yellow wool tie held it shut. Mr. Moss, who had agreed to move garden boxes, saw only the old message and took it by trolley to the garden shed. He went to collect plants before the disappearance was noticed. He never knew he was taking the moon. A rain shower later closed the garden activity tables, moving many identical cartons into the shed and obscuring the moon box. Mr. Moss returns before the solution; he can describe his journey but cannot identify the moon box without the children's reasoning. Adults can contact him; there is no contrived missing phone plot. The wrong label, not deliberate deception, caused the disappearance.

1. The Empty Table: Mila arrives with Dad; librarian Mrs. Bell last saw moon on worktable inside its protective box earlier. A small yellow wool thread is caught on table edge; box went too. A notebook and festival stakes. Establish moon described by Mrs. Bell and a child's earlier involvement making it.
2. Something White: white wheel marks outside library; baker Bea has a white-dusted apron and was delivering trays. Mila leaps toward suspicion while Noah wants to compare marks.
3. The Wrong Question: Mila's question sounds like an accusation and hurts Bea; no public mob. Flour is a tempting guess. Test with water shows trail is pale mud/dried clay (not a universal scientific rule: compare actual samples with the trail). Bea's two-wheel cart fails the measured spacing comparison; front and rear wheel paths may separate during a turn. A repeated gap identifies a damaged wheel, not the number of wheels. Mila apologizes in person. Bea recalls a square box tied with yellow wool on a different trolley.
4. A Wheel with a Bite: borrowed library trolley has a notch in one wheel; compare and roll in damp ground. Mr. Moss borrowed it for garden boxes; he is collecting plants now, Dad sends message and receives confirmation he'll return soon. Following tracks shows a turn into yard, not road. Clue is transport, not guilt.
5. Rain on the Notebook: shower washes some tracks away. Children shelter with Dad, garden volunteer Aunt June explains supplies moved into shed. A partial yellow tie on gate, multiple yellow ties used in garden; not unique proof. Shift from following visible traces to reconstructing choices.
6. The Map That Wasn't: Noah's useful drawing records paths and table positions before rain; Mila had dismissed it as doodling. Combine surviving track with sketch to choose garden route over bakery lane. Mila asks Noah to lead a test; relationship repaired through action.
7. Boxes, Boxes: June opens shed in daylight with Dad; no child locked in. Many reused boxes tied with same wool, all large enough. Check accessible labels. Box marked STARS contains seed pots, establishing reused labels. A shelf/stack cannot be safely lifted by kids; adult will help. Mr. Moss arrives; remembers TO THE GARDEN, did not know moon in box. His statement alone doesn't locate correct box. Stop before obvious reveal.
8. Start Again: use notebook observations vs guesses, no reader lecture. Children retrace library packing. Mrs. Bell says wrote MOON on a short side; borrowed old box from garden. An old message could remain on opposite side. Yellow wool and size narrow boxes; rectangular round-object shape suggested by moon's dimensions. Decide to read every side, including sides against wall.
9. The Other Side: back in shed, adults move boxes while children read all sides; a shallow square box has MOON hidden against wall and TO THE GARDEN visible. Reader can predict. Moon found, with small tear from earlier packing; avoid new major crisis. Reconstruction shows each clue's limited role; no lengthy speech naming every prop.
10. A Moon with a Scar: children and adults repair the small tear using tissue and paste; drying time provides an earned quiet pause, no impossible instant structural fix. Noah's star-shaped patch, Mila wants to hide error but accepts visible repair. Garden volunteers get correct box labels.
11. One Small Light: lantern walk, child detective's satisfaction plus consequences: Bea included, Noah credited; weather clears. Mila chosen to help carry moon with Noah, parent manages support. Some frustration can remain; no universal applause speech. Moon lights safely with battery light; walk through familiar places now transformed.
12. The Last Page: home after walk, gentle close with Dad and notebook, one small domestic lost-object joke paid off within chapter, not a new unresolved central mystery. Finish main emotional arc through concrete action. No sequel requirement.

All chapter prose is drafted in order. Each chapter has four page units, preferably 600–720 words total. Clues: yellow tie (suggestive, shared); pale mud with wheel notch (identifies trolley); sketch and route (garden); labels on reused boxes (explains accident, locates object). Never make an unexplained forensic deduction. Distinguish evidence, testimony and guesses. Characters/readers must see clue before use.
