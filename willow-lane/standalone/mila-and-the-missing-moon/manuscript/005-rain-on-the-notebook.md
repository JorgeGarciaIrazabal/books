# 5. Rain on the Notebook

They reached the narrow roof above the library steps just as the rain became a proper shower.

Dad shook drops from his hair. One went down Mila's neck.

“Sorry,” he said.

Mila held the notebook open. A wet spot had blurred the corner of Noah's drawing. She pressed it carefully against the dry part of her sleeve.

Out in the yard, the pale tracks were turning to watery streaks.

“They're washing away.”

Dad showed her the photograph on his phone. She could see the marks by the doorway, including the little gaps.

But the picture didn't show the whole yard.

Aunt June came hurrying along the garden path. Her coat hung over a basket of paper lanterns instead of over her shoulders.

“Hold this,” she told Dad, giving him the basket.

Then she stood beneath the roof and dripped.

Mila hugged her around the damp middle anyway.

“We've lost the moon,” she said into her aunt's shirt.

Aunt June looked at the empty space where Mila's arms reached around her.

“That's quite a lot of moon to lose.”

Mila explained about the box and the wool, the wheel marks and the trolley.

“Mr. Moss brought some garden boxes over earlier,” Aunt June said. “Then people put more supplies out for the lantern makers. We've just carried everything into the shed to keep it dry.”

Mila lifted her head.

“Did you see our box?”

“I saw several big boxes. I didn't open them. We were trying to beat the rain.”

“Could we look?”

“Of course. Give the shower a few minutes. I need to find the shed key in this enormous pocket.”

She pulled out string, a folded paper bag, and a tiny plastic dinosaur.

“Not that.”

“Why have you got a dinosaur?” Noah asked.

“Excellent question.” She put it on the windowsill. “Perhaps it can guard the basket.”

Mila watched water fall from the roof in silver strings.

When the rain thinned, they walked toward the gate. The air smelled of wet leaves and the mint beside the path.

A small yellow curl hung from the gate latch.

Mila caught Dad's arm.

“More wool!”

It was longer than the thread on the table, with one frayed end. She leaned close, careful of the wet latch.

“The box came this way.”

Aunt June reached into her basket and lifted a bundle of bamboo sticks. Yellow wool held them together.

“Possibly,” she said. “But we've tied lots of things with this wool today. The garden has a whole bag of it.”

Noah turned the bundle until Mila could see its knot.

The wool looked exactly like the scrap on the gate.

Mila let go of Dad's sleeve. A moment ago, the yellow curl had seemed to point straight to the moon.

Now it was only a curl on a gate.

She left it where it hung.

They waited beneath the spreading tree beside the path while Aunt June went to collect a second basket.

Mila watched a drop crawl along the edge of a leaf. At the tip, it grew fat, trembled, and fell onto her shoe.

“We could look in the bakery lane first,” she said.

“Why?” Noah asked.

“It's nearer.”

“The shed's not far.”

“But what if it never went to the shed?”

She was tired of finding things that almost meant something. A white line. A yellow thread. A trolley with no moon on it.

Noah opened the notebook across his palm.

“Can I show you my picture now?”

“It's wet.”

“Only one corner.”

Dad leaned against the tree and waited. He didn't choose a path for them.

Mila looked down at the page she had shut before Noah was finished.

The library door was there. So were the tables.

And so was something she had forgotten.
