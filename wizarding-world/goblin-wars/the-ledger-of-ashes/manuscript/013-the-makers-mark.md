# Chapter Thirteen: The Maker's Mark

Marian Lorne brought paper before breakfast.

Venn's aunt had wrapped it in a shop invoice with the price crossed out. She put it on Sella's bench and asked whether anyone had news of her nephew. Ragna had none. Hedra's second visit had produced a promise to pass on the food and no conversation with either prisoner.

Marian looked at the paper she had brought. “He used to spoil sheets learning his capitals. Now I should like to know if they'll let him have one.”

“You can ask for writing materials with the next package,” Sella said.

“I will.”

She stayed to drink tea standing by the door. The cups were all in use. Orren offered her his, and she told him to finish first. He drank so fast that Sella took it away before he could scald himself.

Issa's letter to the supplier was six lines long. It confirmed the purchase reference, identified Ragna as someone authorised to obtain a copy for the reserve examination, and asked for the supplier's own record of the sale and payment. It restored none of Ragna's old duties. She read it twice anyway, then put it with the advance authority.

Neri carried an empty tool roll. If the foundry had repair work, she meant to ask for it.

“Have you slept?” Sella asked Ragna.

“Yes.”

“Long enough to read a name?”

“Long enough.”

Sella let it pass. She packed bread, a piece of cheese and two apples. Neri took the bag and put the softer apple on top.

Mere Foundry lay upriver, where the water narrowed between wooded banks. They went on foot to save the carrier's fare. Neri knew these Derbyshire lanes from fitting jobs with her father. She took the shorter path past a ruined kiln, then stopped at a new barrier across the track.

Two wizards were checking a charcoal cart. The driver, a goblin in a wide-brimmed hat, had tipped out a sack at their feet. Charcoal dust blew into the hedge.

“It's fuel,” he said. “I've told you. We burn it.”

“You require a stores permit for bulk supplies.”

“Bulk metal. Read the notice.”

The younger wizard read it. He touched the older one's sleeve and pointed to a line. The older man shook him off, then looked at the spilled charcoal again.

“Reload it.”

“Will you hold the sack?”

“Reload it and move on.”

Neri set her bag down. She helped the driver scoop the charcoal from the road. Ragna held the mouth of the sack while he brushed in the last pieces with his hat.

“What is this for?” she asked the younger wizard.

“Movement of restricted stores. After the trouble in the north.”

“What trouble?”

He looked at his companion, then at the two women. “You may proceed on foot.”

The driver tied his sack shut, leaving charcoal in the ditch and an hour of his morning on the road. When Neri offered to help lift it, he said, “No, no. My back's not gone yet,” with a smile that failed halfway across his face.

They went around the cart. Beyond the barrier a notice required carriers of iron to declare their destination and customer before entering the works district. The empty space for a countersignature had been filled by Harrow's office.

Neri waited until they were out of earshot. “He'll put that hour on the delivery bill.”

“He has to eat it somewhere,” Ragna said.

“So do we.”

The road rose above the river, then dipped into the smell of charcoal. Ragna could hear the foundry before she could see it: the steady beat of a hammer and the higher, irregular sound of someone teaching another person to strike in time.

The yard was open to the road. A woman stood on a platform beside a furnace, arguing with a goblin below about the shape of a mould. They both looked up when Neri called.

“Office,” the woman said, pointing with her hammer. “If you want the office, it's there.”

“We need the owner.”

“You've found her. Do you want her to put this down?”

Neri looked at the hot metal waiting in the mould.

“No.”

They waited by a stack of empty frames. The woman and the goblin finished the pour without another word. He moved a lever with both hands; she watched the flow. When the metal settled, he stepped back and made a little bow so mocking that the woman laughed.

Joan Mere washed her hands at the trough before she took Issa's letter.

“What have they done now?” she asked.

Ragna described the separate forty-bar purchase and the missing voucher. She kept the household's other debts out of it.

Mere read the letter again. “They have my receipt. Why don't they show you that?”

“It has been requested. We haven't received it.”

“Have they been paid?” Neri asked.

“The bars? Yes.”

“By whom?”

Mere looked at her. “Who are you?”

Neri gave the workshop name and her own. She did not mention Dorrik until Mere asked whether the older fitter was still doing the gates.

“He's dead.”

Mere lowered the letter. Behind her the hammer kept its slow beat.

“When?”

Neri told her.

“At Blackwater?”

“Yes.”

Mere folded the letter along its crease. “Come in.”

Her office was a room above the store shed. A window looked over the yard, and every impact below came through the floor. The desk held a balance, a bowl of bent nails and three account books bound in different colours. There was a wand in a clay pot beside two quills. Mere used the quill first.

“I will show you this purchase,” she said. “I won't show you my other customers or their prices. Whatever the partners think of one another, they are not getting my trading book copied into their quarrel.”

“That is all we asked.”

“People often ask for one thing and take a look at another while the book is open.”

She put two blank sheets over the adjoining pages before she turned the book towards them.

The invoice was for forty bars. It carried the reference Venn had given and the name of the Works Bank as paying agent. The intended recipient was described as Ministry security stores, via Blackwater lift. Beneath the total was Mere's receipt of payment, dated before the consignment left.

“Cash?” Ragna asked.

“Bank payment order, settled in full. That is the settlement entry.”

“Do you retain the order?”

“The paid one went with the receipt. I keep the counter-entry and the order reference. You can ask the settling office to match them.”

Ragna copied the references with the amount, pausing for Mere to check each one.

“This shows you were paid by the bank,” Ragna said. “It doesn't show which reserve the bank charged.”

“No. That is their account, not mine.”

“And the bars went to Blackwater?”

Mere turned to the dispatch page. “Forty. Weighed here, loaded here. Accepted there.”

The receipt at Blackwater carried a name Ragna did not know: Kelm. Neri leaned over the page.

“The loading foreman,” she said. “He came to see the gate fittings with my father.”

Mere called out of the window. The goblin who had worked the mould looked up. She asked him to come upstairs.

While they waited, Ragna compared the advance authority with the supplier's entry. The purchase, quantity, price and payment reference agreed. The supplier had received the bank's money and sent the bars; that much no longer depended on Ilva's word.

What she could not see was where those bars had gone after the lift.

The goblin came in wiping his hands on a rag. Mere introduced him as Hask, who supervised the bar stock. He examined the dispatch page, then nodded.

“I counted them out.”

“Can you identify them?” Ragna asked.

“If you haven't melted them.”

“What mark?”

He took a small offcut from the shelf. At one end a circle enclosed an M. Beside it were a number and two short cuts crossing at an angle.

“The M is the foundry. The rest is the batch. Don't go looking for any M you fancy and call it this sale.”

Neri took the offcut and turned it in the light. The number was shallow but clean.

“Every bar?”

“Every full bar. On this lot. I was paid to mark it and count it twice.”

“Who asked?”

“The order. Security stock. They wanted to know if somebody changed the iron.”

Ragna copied the exact mark. Hask corrected the angle of one cut. She asked whether the mark could be forged.

“Anything can be copied by somebody who takes enough trouble,” he said. “You'd need to look at the bar, not worship the stamp.”

Neri smiled slightly. It was the first time she had looked at ease since the road.

Mere made a copy of the invoice and the payment entry, covering the other accounts herself. She signed it, and Hask signed a statement identifying the stock he had counted. She would not let Ragna remove the original page.

“I have been paid,” she said. “I still need to prove that if somebody decides I haven't.”

“Will you show it to the partners?”

“If they come here. Or if they pay someone to stand over this yard while I leave it.”

Ragna wrote the condition beside the attestation.

Neri asked about the last part of the journey.

“Did your cart go on from Blackwater?”

“One did,” Mere said. “To Thorn. The load was transferred, but they wanted our narrow cart for the depot passage. They sent an undertaking to return it. I'm still waiting.”

“Your driver?”

“Borr. He came back on the carrier's wagon. They wouldn't let him wait.”

“Did he see the bars arrive?”

“He saw them taken through the gate. He did not count them inside.”

“Can we ask him?”

Mere looked towards the window. “He's gone downriver with another load. He'll be back when he is back.”

Another witness out of reach, another trip. Ragna nearly asked why he hadn't waited, then remembered he had work to do.

“Could you ask him to write what he saw when he returns?”

“I can ask. He may not want his name in it.”

“Then let him decide.”

Mere opened a drawer. “I have permission to collect the cart tomorrow. I was going to send Hask. They say its brake is damaged.”

Neri looked at the sheet she drew out. “Then he should take a fitter.”

Hask stared at her. “You haven't seen the brake.”

“No. Have you?”

“No.”

“Then neither of us knows what we'll need.”

Mere read the collection authority again. It admitted two persons to collect one vehicle from the outer stores. No weapons, no removal of any Ministry material, attendance to be recorded at the gate.

Ragna saw the place name and the thought arrived whole, unpleasantly tempting. A receiving office. A cart. Somewhere beyond it, the record they were not being allowed to inspect.

“We need to see Thorn's receipt,” she said.

“They won't hand you the books with the cart.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Mere put down the authority and looked at her until Ragna felt the lack of sleep behind her eyes.

“This is a foundry, not a cause. People eat because I sell what we make. If you use my cart to carry out Ministry stock, they will close the road to my buyers.”

“We are trying to find a receiving record.”

“Which is Ministry property as well.”

Ragna could not answer that by changing the noun.

Neri set the offcut on the desk. “We can fix the brake. We can look at where the cart is and ask for the record. We won't promise to see nothing. We won't pretend we have permission to take something.”

“And when they say no?”

“We decide what to do then,” Neri said.

Mere leaned back. “You make it sound so easy.”

“It doesn't sound easy to me.”

Hask picked up the collection authority. “The brake wants doing whether they go or I go.”

Mere let out a breath through her nose. She crossed out his name, wrote Neri's and left the second space blank.

“Who else?”

Ragna looked at Neri. The work, the record, her father: she could not tell which had brought Neri this far.

“Me,” Ragna said.

Mere wrote her name.

“You collect the cart. If the brake is beyond repair, you send a message. You do not pledge my foundry, my workers or my family to anything else.”

They agreed. She signed the amended names and gave them a note confirming Neri was engaged to assess the brake. The payment for the job would be small and hers to keep, whether or not the inquiry learned anything from it.

They ate the bread and cheese on the riverbank before walking home. Neri cut her apple with the small knife from her tool roll. Ragna's had softened on one side. She ate the other half and wrapped the rest for later.

“Did you want to say yes before she asked?” Neri said.

“Yes.”

“So did I.”

A boat moved slowly upriver, the man at its stern working against the current. On the bank opposite, two boys were dragging a branch out of the water. Ragna watched them until the branch came free and they both fell backwards, laughing.

“We should tell your mother,” she said.

“I'm going to.”

“I meant what we may be choosing. Not just the repair.”

Neri wiped the knife and folded it away. “So did I.”

Ragna put the supplier's attested copy between two dry boards in her bag. Beneath it lay the collection authority for Thorn, the two new names still darker than the rest of the ink.
