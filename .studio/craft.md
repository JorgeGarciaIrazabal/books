# Craft standards

Start from the author's desired reading experience. A beautiful line cannot repair
a false claim or a character making a choice solely because the outline needs it.

## Author's prose and punctuation preferences

These defaults apply to drafting, translation, and editing across the library.
Follow an explicit author exception when one is given.

- Do not use em dashes in manuscript narration, parenthetical asides, or dialogue.
  Prefer full stops, commas, or a recast sentence as grammar and rhythm require.
  Use quotation marks for speech: double quotation marks in English and guillemets
  («…») in Spanish, with consistent language-appropriate punctuation and clear
  speaker attribution. Spanish dialogue normally permits dialogue dashes; the
  author's house style here chooses quotation marks. Never remove punctuation
  blindly or merge different speakers into one paragraph.
- Avoid stock assistant introductions, canned transitions, repetitive sentence
  openings, repeated contrast formulas, ornamental abstractions, and generic
  emotional gestures. Choose concrete language that fits the character, scene,
  audience, and meaning. Inspect the effect of repeated wording before revising.
- Let dialogue and action carry discoveries and feelings. Remove redundant
  explanations, routine lesson summaries, forced morals, and tidy closing slogans
  when they merely repeat what the scene already shows. Preserve intentional
  repetition and unusual voice when they serve the story.
- Keep assistant disclaimers, writing-process commentary, model signatures,
  watermarks, and unfilled templates out of story text and cover artwork unless
  the author explicitly requests them. Preserve truthful provenance and credits
  in the appropriate records; do not misrepresent how the work was made.
- Treat feedback that prose feels AI-written as a request for specific editorial
  improvements. Do not claim that punctuation identifies authorship, that hidden
  technical watermarks were removed, or that a text will evade an AI detector.
  Do not introduce errors, random wording, or artificial informality to seem human.

## Fiction

- Give scenes a lived situation, a desire, resistance, and a consequential change.
  A quiet conversation can turn a relationship; spectacle is not required.
- Let character choices cause consequences. Test plausible alternatives and why
  this person does not choose them. Antagonists need aims and constraints of their own.
- Ground the viewpoint in selective attention and embodied experience. Maintain
  psychic distance intentionally. A secret in the world bible is not POV knowledge.
- Dialogue is action: competing aims, evasion, subtext, silence, power, and changing
  tactics. Distinct voice comes from worldview and syntax, not catchphrase quotas.
- Make setting affect choices, costs, work, culture, and daily life. Explain only
  what the reader needs at the point they need it. Track the cost of extraordinary powers.
- Vary scene shape, sentence rhythm, and emotional movement. Do not force every
  chapter to end in danger, summarize its lesson, or resolve its emotion explicitly.
- Plant payoffs fairly. Distinguish deliberate ambiguity from missing information.
  Pay the book's central promise even when larger series questions remain open.
- Earn emotion through specificity, conflict, restraint, and consequence. Avoid
  interchangeable gestures, decorative metaphors, repeated realizations, and
  exposition disguised as dialogue. Never use a banned-word list as an editor.
- Preserve intentional strangeness, dialect, humor, genre pleasures, and ambiguity.
  Editing should sharpen the author's voice rather than produce house-model prose.

## Nonfiction

- Specify the reader's starting knowledge, the thesis, and what changes for them.
  Structure each section around a useful question, argument, explanation, or action.
- Map substantive claims to sources actually inspected, at the passage/table level.
  Quote accurately and briefly; distinguish quotation, paraphrase, and interpretation.
- Present the strongest relevant counterargument. Do not promote correlation into
  causation or present anecdotes as representative evidence.
- Mark uncertain, disputed, dated, jurisdiction-specific, and inferred statements.
  Preserve denominators, units, sample boundaries, and source qualifications.
- Label composite or invented examples. Do not invent lived experience or interviews.
- Examples, definitions, diagrams, exercises, and summaries should solve a reader
  problem; use only those that help. Validate worked examples and calculations.

## Read-aloud performance

A read-aloud is a performance of the author's words, not a second draft. Never
change, add or reorder story words to make a line easier to deliver; if the text
resists the voice, revise the casting or the direction, or ask the author.

- The narrator reads the prose, including dialogue tags; characters speak their
  own quoted lines. A full cast should still sound like one book, not a radio
  play fighting for attention.
- Direction is a brief to an actor: emotion, intent, pace, who is being
  addressed. Write it in plain language and place it where the voice should
  change. One direction per turn or emotional beat is usually enough; a
  direction on every line turns performance into recitation.
- Use sounds sparingly. A sigh or laugh earns its place when the story already
  sets it up; it cannot invent a feeling the scene does not contain.
- Pace for a lap and a bedtime, not for an auctioneer. Around 140-160 words per
  minute reads naturally for a picture book, with longer beats at turns and
  shorter ones inside excitement. Speaker-change silences longer than about a
  second and a half start to feel like gaps; decide whether to keep or trim them.
- Pronunciation of names and invented words is part of the book's sound. Record
  the intended form once and reuse it; a homograph that changes meaning ("read",
  "wind", "bow") deserves a note before recording.
- Do not flatten a deliberate oddity to make the voice comfortable. Whispered,
  shouted, singsong or deadpan lines are the author's choices unless the author
  says otherwise.
- Mechanical checks prove words and pacing, not warmth or comic timing. A human
  listen is the only evidence for those, and a synthetic stand-in is a draft, not
  a cast.

## Editing discipline

Read for experience before diagnosis. State where attention, trust, curiosity, or
comprehension changes and cite the text. Offer a few high-impact changes, not a
laundry list. Protect successful passages and distinguish intentional effects from
accidents. One meaningful structural experiment is better than endless abstract debate.

For close imitation requests, translate references into broad craft traits and
develop an original voice. Track permissions for quotes, images, and commissioned
assets in the production brief. A model is not a substitute for a real lived-experience
reader, credentialed expert, permissions owner, or human proofreader when one is needed.
