# Chapter Nine: The Abstract

The relief fund bought bread in the morning and had nothing left for coal.

Fenn counted the coins with Ragna and Sella watching. There were three knuts after he paid the baker. He pushed them into the middle of the cloth so that nobody would have to ask whether he had overlooked one.

“People can eat together tonight,” he said. “Two fires instead of seven.”

“People are already eating together,” Sella replied.

“Then one fire.”

Vessa came in carrying a sack of kindling. “Brann split this. He says it isn't much use for the lift, so perhaps we may keep it.”

It was clean scrap from a cart repair, paid for out of his own wages the previous month. He had brought the bill in case anyone asked. Ragna wanted to laugh and could not find a way to do it without hurting him.

They decided where the kindling would go. Vessa took the list. Before she left, she said a chain tester had been sent home from Blackwater for refusing to sign the attendance report.

“Paid?” Fenn asked.

“She wasn't told.”

“Tell her to bring her tally.”

“She hasn't got it. They kept it in the office.”

Ragna closed the empty purse. Another account to reconstruct, another person asked to remember exact hours after someone else had taken the record. She wrote the woman's name on the sheet headed agreed and disputed, then left the amount blank.

The meeting began at eleven. People came from the furnaces on their meal break and stood along the back wall. Brelk had work across the river and sent a message with Edda: he could not come, but he wanted to know what happened. She brought his message on the back of a bill.

Ragna read Harrow's answer first. She read it without comments. When she reached the sentence about missing materials, Neri said, “Say that again.”

Ragna did.

“They know where some of it went,” Neri said. “They wrote it down.”

“We have a clerk's statement and a dispatch copy,” Ragna replied. “We don't have the receiving record.”

“Then ask for that, in those words.”

She wrote it beneath the request already refused.

Issa's letter came next. People listened more quietly to that one. An examination of the agreed balances, no promise of payment, two partners required to sign. Ragna described what Arven had compared and certified. She showed the outside of the wrapper without opening the household pages.

“Why isn't Arven here?” someone asked.

“He's at work.”

“So are we.”

“He signed what he had checked. He hasn't agreed to speak for the claim.”

Fenn stood. “I have. As far as the copies agree with the dispatch I saw. Beyond that, don't put words in my mouth.”

Daniel Venn sat on the bench nearest the door. His aunt had come with him, wearing a hat whose ribbon had faded purple in the sun. He had asked her to stay away. She had asked whether he intended to pay her for advice and, when he said no, had come anyway.

He stood before Ragna could call on him.

“I changed the destination on a dispatch for twenty bars,” he said. “The instruction came through Mr Harrow's office. I knew no notice had been sent to the workshop. The altered record is mine. I kept a duplicate when the other copies were called in.”

A furnace worker at the back asked, “Did you know we were paying for it?”

“I knew whose material it was. I didn't know what had been charged.”

“You could have asked.”

“Yes.”

The worker waited. Venn did not offer anything else. After a while the man folded his arms and leaned against the wall again.

Ragna asked whether Venn had lodged his statement.

“Yesterday afternoon. They accepted a signed copy. I kept the dispatch original because I was told I might be required to produce it.”

“Who accepted it?”

“The receiving clerk. I have the receipt.”

He showed it to Fenn, who read out the date and reference. No one applauded. Venn sat down with his hands between his knees. His aunt touched his sleeve once, then let him be.

Sella asked that the family's account of the delivery be read next. Neri had written it herself. Twenty bars had arrived; she and Sella had counted them; the workshop had not authorised another destination. The statement did not claim that Dorrik had understood what he signed.

“Leave his name on it,” Sella said. “It is part of what happened. But don't say he saw things we cannot ask him about.”

They agreed to add the observed state of the rail separately. The date of the observation went at the top in large writing. Neri insisted on it. She did not want yesterday's drawing turned into a picture of the night he died.

Tavin had made a short abstract from the letters and the statements. It fit on one side of a sheet. He gave it to Ragna before he stood up.

“We can't give everybody all the papers,” he said. “This says what the claims are.”

Ragna read it. At first she was relieved. The household debts were absent. It described a disputed iron charge, a redirected consignment, a suspended wage payment and the request for a hearing. It named neither Venn's mother nor the people receiving relief. It named Dorrik because Sella had agreed.

It also included the dispatch reference.

“Why put that in?” she asked.

“So they know it's a real dispatch.”

“Venn agreed to use of the facts, not public display of his name.”

“His name isn't there.”

“It can tell his office which paper we're using.”

“His office has his statement.”

Venn looked up. “It does.”

“Knowing isn't the same as seeing it on a door,” Ragna said.

Tavin pressed his palms against the table. “Everybody here has been asked for names and signatures. The people who took the book keep sending letters without names. When do we get to say what we're asking for?”

“You can say it now.”

“To the same room.”

Fenn took the abstract. He read it twice, then handed it to Sella.

“We should say it outside the room,” he said. “But it ought to be a claim, not a verdict.”

The meeting changed three sentences. “Stolen” became “charged without evidence of delivery”. Harrow's inquiry was described as refusing a separate hearing, not refusing every possible hearing. The financial advance was left out because Ilva had not yet made her statement to the partners. The abstract said further records had been requested.

Ragna asked Venn about the dispatch reference. He rubbed his chin, leaving a pale mark where his fingers pressed.

“Use the number,” he said at last. “It's the instruction I changed.”

“Not your name?”

“Not on a wall. Not yet.”

Tavin wrote the dispatch number on the fair copy. The nib caught, leaving a blot at the end of the line.

They agreed that the statement should go with the partners' request. Copies for the workshops would follow after the bank meeting the next morning. A man from the furnaces wanted them out at once. Sella argued for giving the partners a chance to examine the balances without having to speak over a crowd. Edda said the furnaces needed an answer before the next shift.

“There may not be an answer tomorrow,” Ragna said.

“Then why wait?”

Nobody had a satisfying answer. In the end the room agreed to a delay because Issa had offered something specific and Harrow had not. Ragna wrote the decision at the bottom of the meeting sheet.

People went back to work or to the hunt for it. Sella took the food list. Fenn went with Vessa to distribute the kindling. The empty room smelled of damp coats and the cold ashes from its little stove.

Venn stayed behind while his aunt found her gloves.

“My sister sent a note,” he said to Ragna. “Mother wants the blue footstool. She says she forgot it because we all hurried her.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

He smiled for the first time. It barely moved his mouth.

“I can take it tonight,” he said. “Ruth doesn't live far.”

“Will you be at the bank meeting?”

“If they're taking statements.”

“They have agreed to examine wages. I don't know what else they'll hear.”

“Then I'll bring the dispatch and ask.”

He went out with his aunt. Ragna gathered the consented papers and left the room locked behind her.

The afternoon brought a small success. Arven had found the withdrawal slip that explained the doubtful advance on one of Tavin's balances. The amount was smaller than the household remembered. Ragna went with Tavin to compare it against the worker's tally. The man checked his own figures, swore, and signed a correction. He had added one week twice.

“You'd think I could remember taking money,” he said.

“I remembered entering a delivery,” Ragna replied. “We both had to look again.”

They walked back through the market. A basket seller was folding up his stall. At the far end, beside the public pump, six people stood around a sheet fastened to a post.

Ragna recognised the heading before she could read the words.

Tavin stopped beside her.

“How many?” she asked.

“Six.”

“Where?”

“Here. The two furnace gates. The carrier's yard. The lower market. Hirk took one.”

Ragna looked at him. He did not look away.

“We agreed tomorrow.”

“Edda was going to tell the shift anyway. She asked for the words. I gave them to her.”

“And the others?”

“I copied them.”

“You made a decision for everyone who waited.”

“I gave them what they agreed to say.”

“After the bank meeting.”

“They have an office, Ragna. They can change what they do before breakfast. We have a meal break and a room people can only come to if they miss work.”

She wanted a quick answer. He was right about the meal break and the room. Her anger could do nothing with that.

“They chose the delay,” she said. “You don't get to decide their reason wasn't good enough.”

He looked towards the post. “I know.”

“Do you?”

“I know what I did.”

People were beginning to notice them. She let the apology wait.

The abstract had drawn a correction in someone else's hand: WHO WILL READ THE COPY IF THEY TAKE THIS TOO? Below it a second person had written: MAKE ANOTHER.

Ragna read Tavin's text from beginning to end. The room's words, every one of them. He had changed no fact, added no private name. She read the last line again before speaking.

“Leave this one,” she said when he reached towards it.

He lowered his hand.

“We can't call it private now. Go and tell Sella exactly where they are.”

“And you?”

“I am going to tell Venn.”

She found him at the baker's door with the blue footstool under his arm. He listened without interrupting. When she finished, he looked down the street towards the Ministry court.

“They would know the number,” he said. “Whether there was a copy outside or not.”

“Yes.”

“Will it help?”

“I don't know.”

“Nor do I.”

He put the footstool back inside the door. “I'd better come with you.”

“We can meet tomorrow.”

“I don't want to wait in an empty house tonight.”

At the weigh house, Sella had returned to count the food receipts. Tavin stood beside her. She was not speaking to him, but she had given him figures to add. Ragna could not tell whether that was forgiveness or simply work that needed doing.

Fenn arrived soon after, carrying a copy of the abstract from the lower market. Someone had read it aloud at the furnace gate. The late shift wanted to send four people to hear the partners' answer in the morning. By the time the others heard this, two more workshops had asked the same thing.

“Not everybody in the court,” Sella said.

“Tell them,” Fenn replied. “They've waited a week.”

There was a knock at the outer door. Tavin moved to open it. Before he reached the latch, a voice called Daniel Venn's name.

Venn stood up. His aunt was not with him now. He put a hand inside his coat, where the dispatch duplicate lay.

Pell came in with Harrow behind him. Two more wizards waited in the doorway. Harrow carried a flat black case, smaller than the one Merrok had brought with the relief money.

“Mr Venn,” he said. “You have retained an official record after being directed to surrender it.”

“I have offered it to the inquiry.”

“You will accompany us and produce it.”

Sella rose from the table. Ragna put the receipt from the receiving office beside the food list, where Harrow could see it.

“He lodged a statement. The record is available to be examined here.”

Harrow looked at the sheet fastened to the wall. It was another copy of the abstract. Someone had brought it in from the rain.

“This is not an examination room,” he said.

Outside, footsteps stopped on the stone. Ragna heard someone ask whether the meeting had started again. More people were arriving, called by the light in the windows and the sight of four wizards at the door.

Tavin stood beside the latch. He had not moved back to his seat.
