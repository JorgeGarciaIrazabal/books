# Chapter Twenty-Two: The Work of Keeping

The first person Sella went to pay was not at home.

Sella found him at his cousin's forge, working a bellows with his coat off. He saw the envelope in her hand and stopped. The fire sank behind him.

“I wasn't sure you'd want me coming here,” she said.

“I wasn't sure you'd pay me.”

She gave him the envelope. He counted the coins on a ledge, once, and then made her wait while he found his own record of the days. His cousin worked the bellows in his place.

“You've included the afternoon I left.”

“You showed Neri where the hinge would bind.”

“That took ten minutes.”

“It saved her more.”

He put the last coin into his purse. “Tell her the new pin wants filing on the inside edge. I forgot to say.”

Sella did not ask whether he would come back. She took his receipt and went to the next address on her list.

By noon the workshop table was covered with signed slips. A few people could not be found, and one disputed the number of hours Sella had recorded. They sat with the tally sticks between them until they found a day entered under the wrong week. Neither remembered who had made the mark. Sella paid the difference and wrote the correction where both of them could see it.

Neri sat at the bench with her foot on a padded stool. The healer had allowed her to work if she kept it raised and stopped when the swelling returned. She had brought the broken warning bell to the bench and taken its little striker apart.

“It doesn't know the difference between a thief and a coat,” Orren said.

“It knows the difference between a coat and a door.”

“It caught the healer.”

“She walked into the door.”

Orren considered this. “Perhaps it should ask first.”

Neri put a spring in his hand. “Ask it how.”

Ragna arrived with Tavin. He was wearing the other coat, the one he preferred. The bandage on his wrist had been replaced with a smaller one, and he had eaten breakfast at home, though Hedra had sent bread with him in case he had lied.

He put the bread on the table. “My mother says it's for anyone who needs it.”

“Does she know you didn't eat it?” Sella asked.

“She watched me wrap it.”

“Then we may safely take it.”

He sat beside Ragna with the copied wage schedule. They had agreed to help Arven reconstruct the pages of the iron ledger that had burned. Some figures survived in workshop books, withdrawal slips and receipts. Others did not. Arven had ruled a separate column for sums no one could yet prove. He had also left space beside each entry for the person affected to write a disagreement.

“It will make the book untidy,” he had said.

“Good,” Sella had replied.

The first disagreement came before lunch. A woman from the furnace believed she had repaid a small loan in winter. The bank slip showed only half the sum. Her brother remembered carrying the rest to the counting house, but could not remember which clerk had taken it.

Ragna found no receipt, and the brother had written no other record. The woman wanted the loan marked paid; the bank would not yet agree.

“What do you put there?” she asked, pointing to the new column.

“Both accounts,” Ragna said. “Yours and the bank's. With the dates we can establish.”

“And I still owe it?”

“The bank says you do. You say you don't. We haven't resolved that.”

The woman looked at the page. “Will it stop me taking my wages?”

“Not the wages already agreed. Issa has signed that.”

“Then write it.”

The woman read Ragna's entry aloud and changed one word: her brother had carried the money to the counting house. She could not name the clerk who took it.

When they had finished, Tavin took the sheet to Arven at the bank house. He had been offered his apprenticeship back, but not under Ragna. Merrok's suspension remained in place while the partners examined the accounts. Arven had asked Tavin to help with the reconstruction because he knew where the copies were kept. Tavin had agreed for one week. He had not agreed to say nothing about what happened after it.

Ragna's own employment ended with a letter delivered that afternoon. Issa had signed it. The bank could not retain her as an authorised examiner while she was a witness in the inquiry, the letter said. Her wages would be paid through the end of the month. There was a separate offer of six weeks' work advising the partners on reconstructed accounts. It paid by the day, gave her no authority to certify a balance and could be ended with a week's notice.

Sella read the letter after Ragna.

“Will you take it?”

“I don't know.”

“You don't have to answer today.”

“I know.”

Sella folded the letter and gave it back. This time she did not correct the answer.

Mere came the next morning with Neri's tool roll. The small file with the chipped handle was missing. The hammer and pliers were there, along with two pins that did not belong to Neri.

“The depot sent the cart back,” Mere said. “The rim is worse. They say they aren't paying for it.”

“Did you get that in writing?” Ragna asked.

Mere gave her a look. “Do you think I went there without paper?”

Neri untied the roll. She picked up the hammer, turned it and put it down. Then she counted the tools again.

“I can replace the file,” Sella said.

“I know.” Neri pushed the extra pins towards Orren. “These aren't ours.”

He did not take them. “Can we ask whose?”

“Yes. After I ask about my file.”

Mere had a second envelope. It contained a copy of the depot's refusal to cover the cart hire. She placed it beside the counterfoil, which had been returned to her after the partners' examination.

“I have a claim against Thorn. I mean to keep it separate from yours.”

“Of course,” Sella said.

“I didn't say I wouldn't show them together. I said they are separate.”

She stayed for tea. Kelm was working at her foundry under Hask's supervision. He had given Vale a statement about the lift, including his own decision to remove the rail and continue the work. Vale had taken a separate statement from Brann, who knew only what happened below. She had also asked Pell for the account Kelm said he had been shown at Thorn.

“Will there be a hearing?” Sella asked.

“They say there will.” Mere stirred her tea. “They asked Kelm whether he had anything to add. He said he would answer questions when they put them in writing.”

“Did Harrow give a statement?”

“I wasn't there.”

Sella looked at the table for a long time. “I want what we know about Dorrik put where people can read it. Not every detail. Enough that they stop saying he went below alone.”

Neri set down the bell striker. “Can I hear the rest first?”

Sella looked at her. “Yes.”

They went to the healer's room that evening because it was quiet and Neri could keep her foot supported there. Ragna came at Neri's request and brought Kelm's signed account. Sella sat on one side of the bed and held a cup of water. The healer closed the curtain and told them to call if they needed her.

Ragna read from the place where she had stopped before. She read how Kelm removed the rail, how Dorrik objected, how Harrow told them to continue and the load shifted. She read Kelm's uncertainty about what Dorrik intended when he reached for the cart. She read that Kelm had shut the control and that Brann had called from below.

Neri listened with her hands on the blanket. At the end she asked Ragna to read the sentence about the brake again.

“He asked my father to look at it.”

“Yes.”

“He didn't go down there by himself.”

“No.”

Neri took the water from Sella and drank. Then she asked for the account. Ragna gave it to her and waited while she read the pages herself. Her eyes stopped at Kelm's signature.

“Will he come if I ask?”

“I think so,” Sella said. “You don't have to ask.”

“I know.”

She handed the account back. “Not yet.”

They wrote the public notice two days later. Sella chose the words; Neri changed one sentence; Ragna checked the dates against the statements. It said that Dorrik had been at the lift while a load was being moved, that the corner rail had been removed, and that he had fallen after the load shifted. Kelm and Brann had each agreed to these limited descriptions before the notice was posted. It identified their roles without naming either witness: the foreman at the lift and the porter who found Dorrik below. It said the circumstances remained under inquiry. It did not claim that a court had decided responsibility.

Sella took the first copy to the workshop. Neri fastened it beside the brass plate with the four names. She could not stand for long, so Orren held the paper while she worked the pins into the wood.

“Is it straight?” he asked.

“No,” she said.

He moved his hand. “Now?”

“Close enough.”

The Ministry's reserve order had not been withdrawn. Vale returned to the bank house to take statements about the fire and the detention. Harrow was no longer directing that examination; Ragna heard he had been assigned elsewhere while his actions were reviewed. When people asked whether he had been dismissed, she said she hadn't heard.

Venn made his own statement about the altered dispatch. Marian went with him and waited outside the room. When he came out, she gave him a parcel of paper and told him it was not a gift. He paid her the following week, in two instalments, and she entered both in her shop book.

Ilva left her position at the bank. She gave Issa the keys to the examiner's cabinet and signed an inventory of what remained in it. She did not ask Ragna to speak for her. At the next meeting of the contributors she stood and described the advance, the second charge and the page she had concealed. Some wanted her barred from the room; others had more questions. She stayed to hear them all and accepted that the next meeting would decide whether she could return.

The news from outside the district came in pieces. A carrier reported a road closed farther north. A furnace worker said his brother's workshop had stopped a Ministry order. The next day someone said the brother had gone back to work. By week's end, no one at Sella's table could say whether that workshop was working or idle.

Tavin wanted to print the district's account of the affair. Ragna wanted to wait until the hearing had taken evidence from Kelm and Brann. They argued in the workshop while Neri sorted the returned tools.

“If we wait for every official to finish asking, they'll write the only account people have,” Tavin said.

“If you publish a witness's name before he agrees, you may make him unable to give the account at all.”

“I didn't say I would.”

“You didn't ask yet.”

He set the sheet on the bench. “Then come with me when I ask.”

She looked at the sheet. It named the bank, the Ministry order, the duplicate charge and the payments. There were blank spaces where he had intended to add the witness accounts. At the bottom he had written that the inquiry remained open.

“All right,” she said.

They went first to Mere's foundry. Kelm was working beside Hask, carrying finished fittings to a rack. He listened to the request and asked to read the sheet. He kept it until the end of the shift. When he returned it, he had crossed out his own name and written a sentence that identified his role without giving his address.

“You can use that,” he said. “If the hearing asks for me, I'll answer for myself.”

Brann agreed to a shorter description of what he had seen at the bottom of the shaft. Vessa read it over before he signed. Borr did not agree to publish his name, but allowed the account to say that a carrier had brought twenty bars to Thorn. Rell still refused to show her load number. Tavin left that part blank.

They printed the sheet at Marian's shop. She charged for the paper and allowed them to use the press after closing. Ragna fed the pages while Tavin took them from the other side. The first three came out crooked. Marian made them stop, adjust the guide and begin again. She did not offer to do the work herself.

When they had finished, Tavin set one copy aside for the bank house and another for the school. He took six for the furnace gates, then looked at Ragna.

“Tomorrow?”

“What did the meeting agree?”

“Tomorrow, after the people named have seen it.”

“Then tomorrow.”

He put the six copies back on the table. There was ink on the side of his hand. He tried to wipe it away and only spread it.

The following morning Ragna went to Sella's workshop with the last of the reconstructed wage entries for the week. The warning bell sounded as she came in, then stopped before it caught her coat. Neri had fitted a smaller spring. Orren stood beneath it with the wooden boat, watching for another failure.

“It worked,” he said.

“For her,” Neri replied. “We haven't tried it on you.”

He went out and came back through the door three times. On the third attempt the bell gave a short, offended note. He looked pleased.

Sella cleared a place at the table. Ragna laid down the entries, including the disputed loan. Beside them she put a copy of Tavin's account, now marked with the permissions each witness had given. Sella read the loan entry first.

“We'll need another page,” she said. “There isn't room for the woman's reply.”

Ragna fetched one from her bag. She had started carrying loose paper again. The pencil was shorter than it had been at the beginning of the quarter, and she had wrapped thread around the end to make it easier to hold.

Outside, a cart stopped in the yard. Someone called for a hinge to be examined. Neri told Orren to ask which door it belonged to before bringing it inside. He went up the passage with the boat still in his hand.

Ragna turned to the clean page and wrote the woman's name. Then she left enough space for the answer.
