# Chapter Twenty-One: Two Signatures

The torn edges did not quite meet.

A triangle of paper had burned away between the two pieces. Arven placed them on a board and held a ruler beside the surviving lines. The red ink on one piece ended with the first half of a reference; the other began with the final numbers. They matched the control book. The missing paper still left room for a challenge.

“Could there have been another entry between them?” Halrik asked.

“There isn't room for one,” Ilva said.

“Could the reference have been altered?”

“Anything could be altered.” Arven looked at him. “Do you want to know whether this was?”

“Yes.”

“Then I want the other copies.”

Ilva brought the statement she had signed at Sella's workshop. Ragna fetched the attested copy of the advance authority. Mere laid the receiving counterfoil beside the board, far enough from the damp edge that it would not be damaged. Borr's statement lay in a separate wrapper: evidence of the earlier twenty, not this purchase.

They worked in the back room while the Ministry officers counted the reserve boxes again. Vale had ordered the street door kept open. The people who had come to see the transfer remained outside under umbrellas and pieces of canvas. Someone passed cups of hot water through the crowd. It was all the kitchen had left after the tea ran out.

Ilva put her fingertip beside the surviving part of Merrok's signature.

“I covered this page,” she said. “I saw the entry before I did it. The advance was forty bars at the supplier's rate. The recovery was posted as a second household charge for the same amount.”

“Did Merrok tell you to enter it?” Issa asked.

“He told me to conceal the reconciliation. He said the posting had been made under his instruction. I did not see who wrote the household entry.”

Merrok sat at the far end of the table. He had asked for a clean cloth and had wiped the soot from his face. The mark remained in the seam of his collar.

“You told me it would be reversed,” Ilva said to him.

“It was intended to be.”

“Did you instruct the charge?”

He looked at the torn page, then towards the yard where Harrow had claimed the fire began during an obstruction. “Yes. Harrow will leave the posting to me, won't he?”

Arven stopped moving the ruler. Halrik took up his pen.

“Write it exactly,” Merrok said. “I instructed a temporary posting against the household account. The first forty-bar release made a matching charge easier to hide. The Ministry was to repay the advance.”

“Did the household consent?” Sella asked.

“No.”

“Did you tell them?”

“No.” He rubbed the edge of the table with a thumb. “I had a written undertaking from Harrow's office. I believed the money would return before the quarter closed.”

“And when it didn't?”

“The Ministry required the ledger.”

“You signed for it to go.”

“I signed because the order had already been made.”

Ragna had heard him invoke the order before. It existed; so did the duplicate charge and the page he had concealed. Now his own account connected them.

Halrik read back what he had written. Merrok corrected one phrase, replacing “payment promised” with “undertaking received”. He signed the correction and the statement beneath it. Ilva signed her own account. Arven and the Ministry clerk, returned from the healer with his ankle bound, witnessed both signatures.

“Now the wages,” Sella said.

Issa took the two surviving pieces of the reconciliation and placed them beside the control book. She did not touch the burned edges.

“The second charge should not have been made,” she said. “The earlier charge for twenty bars sent to Thorn is supported by Venn's dispatch, Borr's account and the household books. We can authorise payment against those findings.”

Halrik looked at Merrok. “The Ministry owes the reserve for the forty.”

“Yes.”

“If it refuses?”

“We pursue the undertaking.”

“And until then?”

Merrok did not answer.

Halrik turned to Sella. “The bank must meet the balance from its working reserve. We will enter the Ministry claim separately. Your household will not carry it while we wait.”

Sella sat down. She had been standing since she brought the rail statement into the hall. Ragna drew the stool closer.

The calculation took another hour. The earlier twenty had been put aside when the partners paid Sella the previous afternoon. They now removed the second forty-bar charge from the household account. Halrik checked that the earlier partial payment was credited. Issa checked the remaining wages and withdrawals. Arven wrote a schedule showing what had been paid, what remained due and which claims were still open.

“And those who signed Merrok's relief form?” Edda asked.

Halrik read the form. “The payment is credited. The release says the bank's determination applies. The bank is determining that the charge was wrong.”

“Can you put that where the clerk who took my signature can see it?”

“I can put it on your receipt.”

“My aunt?”

“The allowance is a separate decision. I will examine the register and hear what she can show about when she moved in.”

Edda considered this. “She has a rent receipt. The roof leaked through it.”

“Bring what is left.”

Halrik and Issa signed the authorisation together. The clerk brought the working reserve case from the locked cabinet. There was not enough cash in it to pay every verified balance at once. The partners signed orders on their London account for the remainder and arranged for the first carrier to take them that evening. Arven read the dates aloud. A furnace worker asked what would happen if the carrier did not arrive.

“Then the bank still owes the money,” Issa said. “And you come to us.”

“Where?”

“Here.”

She gave the worker a copy of the signed schedule. He folded it and put it inside his shirt.

Outside, the rain had eased. Tola came from Blackwater with news that the second chain tester had agreed to stop work until the detainees were accounted for. A Ministry officer had offered to sign the lift himself; neither tester would certify the lower chain alone. At the bank house, the hired carters had separately refused to load the reserve boxes until the detainees were accounted for. One feared losing his Ministry contract and had asked his brother to seek other work for him. Vale could seek replacement drivers, but could not assemble them immediately.

Vale listened to the report without asking Tola to repeat it for the other officers.

“You are withholding work to influence a Ministry inquiry,” she said.

“Yes,” Tola replied.

“Are you preventing anyone else from entering the works?”

“No.”

Vale looked at the waiting carts. “I have sent to Blackwater for the detention records.”

“When?” Hedra asked.

“An hour ago.”

“And when will they come?”

“I cannot tell you.”

Hedra took her son's package receipt from her pocket. It had become soft at the folds. She laid it on the table in front of Vale.

“Pell signed this. It says my son is there. You can send another person if the first hasn't found the door.”

Vale read the receipt. She called the officer from the street and gave him a written instruction. He left on horseback.

Harrow had remained in the yard after surrendering his wand. When Vale came out, he asked to speak to her alone. She took him into the covered passage, where Ragna could see them but not hear. Merrok waited in the hall. Once, he began towards the passage; Issa told him to stay.

“You cannot suspend my authority on the word of a crowd,” he said.

“I am not doing it on their word.” She held up his signed statement. “You directed a charge against a household that did not consent. You concealed the Ministry debt from the contributors.”

“I told you why.”

“You did. We will include it.”

Halrik took the key to the London accounts from Merrok's chain. Merrok unfastened it himself. The chain left a red mark on his wrist. He put his hand over the mark and looked at the wall while Issa wrote the temporary removal of his signing authority.

“This is pending a full examination,” she said.

“I heard you.”

The horse returned before dusk. Vale read the message it carried, then sent the rider back with a second instruction. Hedra stood beside Ragna while they watched him leave.

“What did it say?” she called.

Vale hesitated. “Mr Tavin is recorded as held for obstruction. Mr Venn for retention and alteration of an official record. Neither has been brought before the reviewing officer.”

“Bring them here,” Hedra said.

“I have ordered their release into the custody of the inquiry clerk while the allegations are reviewed.”

“Custody?”

“Accompanied here. They will not be kept at Blackwater.”

Hedra looked at Ragna. Neither of them knew whether the wording would permit the men to walk into the house or stop them at the door. Vale wrote another instruction, this time in Hedra's presence. It named both men and directed that they be brought to the bank house without bindings. It also directed that Venn's wand be returned to him unless an officer recorded a specific reason to retain it.

“Will you sign that?” Hedra asked.

Vale did.

They waited. Fenn went home with Vessa; he had been standing too long and his hand had begun to throb. Edda took a meal to her aunt. Sella stayed with Hedra, though Neri was alone at the workshop with a neighbour. Mere put the counterfoil back in its oilcloth and gave the pieces of the reconciliation to Issa under a receipt. The bank would keep them with the control book. Arven made copies before the ink on the receipt dried.

At last a cart came up the road. It was not the cart that had taken Tavin. This one had a flat bed and no rear gate. Tavin sat beside the driver. Venn sat behind them with an officer, holding his wand in both hands as if he had forgotten where to put it.

Hedra reached the cart before it stopped. Tavin climbed down badly, his legs stiff. She caught him and held him so tightly that he made a noise into her coat. She loosened her grip, examined the bandage on his wrist and then held him again.

Marian Lorne came from the crowd. Venn got off the cart without help. He gave her the wand to hold while he put on his coat, then took it back and tucked it away.

Ragna stood a little distance from Tavin. She let the things she had prepared to say wait until he loosened his hold on his mother.

When he finally looked up, he said, “Did you get the book?”

“Parts of it.”

“Was anyone hurt?”

“Brann burned his fingers. The clerk's ankle. The healer has seen him.”

Tavin looked past her at the smoke mark over the doorway. “Did it work?”

“We have enough for the partners to pay the disputed charge. They signed.”

“And Venn?”

“He's here.”

“I can see that. What happens to him?”

Vale approached with the officer who had accompanied the cart. The allegations against both men remained open, she said. They were required to provide addresses where notices could reach them. They were free to leave the bank house and would receive a written summons if further questions were required.

Tavin gave Hedra's address. Venn gave Marian's shop, after asking her. She agreed and told the officer the hours when he could deliver a notice without blocking her customers.

Hedra took her son home. Marian went with Venn towards the baker's. The remaining workers began to discuss when the lift would be tested. Tola said she would return in the morning if she could examine the whole chain with another tester and if the foreman would stop calling the halt an act of sabotage. She would not promise on anyone else's behalf.

The carts for the reserve records stayed empty. Vale sealed the boxes in the bank house with Issa and Arven witnessing the inventory. She set a date for a new hearing on the transfer and the fire. The records remained under order. Their owners knew where they were and what each box held; Ragna had hoped to bring them back to the counting room.

As Ragna left, Sella caught up with her and put an envelope in her hand.

“What's this?”

“Your coal. From the fund.”

“I can pay it back when my wages come.”

“You can give to the fund when you have something to give.”

Ragna opened the envelope. Inside was a note naming a coal merchant and the amount the trustees had agreed. It was not enough for the whole winter. She folded it along its crease and put it in her pocket.
