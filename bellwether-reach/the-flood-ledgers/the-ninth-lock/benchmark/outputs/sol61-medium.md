## P1

A city has sold the same safety twice. Sera Venn can prove that both certificates are authentic, but authenticity cannot make the Ninth Lock protect two basins. To expose the sale before the tide, she must obtain the second original from Ivo Renn, the former apprentice who has reasons to distrust her even when she is right. Her first effective intervention will preserve North’s protection—and put her own brother among its beneficiaries.

The book’s distinctive pleasure is following a practical inspector through an honest-looking chain of records whose individual entries conceal an impossible promise. The mystery asks who arranged that chain, who profited, and which ordinary acts kept it working.

**Proposed local custom:** households mark the height of their last flood on the outside of their doors and leave the mark after repairs. An inspector taking testimony must therefore walk past what earlier assignments cost. Neighbors can also see whose account of a flood has changed. The custom supplies witnesses and social pressure, never a magical test of honesty.

The repeatable series engine is a disputed civic obligation with a physical consequence: each book follows a different document through the people who issue, honor, evade, and depend on it. Each dispute receives its own answer.

The emotional promise is competence made answerable to others. Sera and Ivo may learn to work together without receiving either automatic forgiveness or a consequence-free victory.

## P3

Ivo set the South certificate under his palm. Sera could see the corner of its seal.

“Lift your hand.”

“You used to say please when visitors were here.”

“There aren’t any.”

“No.”

She laid the North certificate on the desk with her right hand. Her splint caught the edge, pushing it crooked. Ivo straightened it without moving his other hand.

Two papers. She had inspected machinery with fewer precautions.

“I need the original,” she said. “A copy won’t establish what was registered.”

“I know what a copy establishes.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

“You never had to.”

Sera looked toward the clock. The movement was small enough that she could have pretended she hadn’t made it. He took his palm off the seal and laid it farther up the paper.

“You can write a complaint afterward,” she said.

“Where would you like me to put it?”

There was an empty chair beside her. She had been making him look up all this time.

She sat. Her wrist knocked against the arm, and for a moment the only useful thing she could do was breathe through her nose.

Ivo waited. He could always wait longer than she could.

“Keep a receipt,” she said. “If you release it to me. Write that I took it, and why.”

“And if it becomes inconvenient that you took it?”

“Keep the receipt.”

He reached for his pen.

She let him finish the first line before she spoke again.

“I should have kept yours.”

The pen stopped. He did not look up.

After a while he turned the receipt so she could read it. His hand still rested on the South certificate, but now it covered only the blank margin.

## P4

The Registry clock said five. Sera put the North certificate on Ivo’s desk and kept her right hand on it until he looked up.

“You have another.”

“I have several thousand.”

“For the Ninth Lock.”

He moved his chair back. A drawer stood open against his knee. She could see paper inside, nothing she could identify.

“I need to see it,” she said.

“In what capacity?”

She nearly said his inspector. The words would have been wrong in more than one direction.

“As a licensed inspector.”

“Then you know how to dispute an assignment.”

Her left wrist throbbed inside the splint. She eased it against her stomach. Getting the North paper out had been awkward enough; she had no spare hand for holding a drawer open or stopping him closing it.

“I haven’t disputed anything yet.”

“You’ve come to my desk and put your hand over a certificate. What would you call it?”

“A beginning.”

Ivo looked at her hand until she took it away.

He had learned to handle paper from her. No wet fingers. No folds across a seal. If the sheet resisted, find what held it before pulling. She watched him lift a certificate from the drawer with exactly that care.

He laid it beside North’s and kept two fingers on its upper edge.

South Basin. The same high tide. The same lock.

Sera bent closer. The seal’s impression held cleanly in the paper. She checked the North seal again, though she had checked it twice before coming. Both authentic. Neither could make room for the other.

“You registered this.”

“Yes.”

“And this.”

“Yes.”

She waited. Ivo looked back at her.

“What am I supposed to tell South?” she asked.

“That isn’t a question for the clerk.”

“It becomes one when the clerk registers two protections for one tide.”

“Write that down.”

There was enough anger in his voice to make her straighten. He did not explain it, and she could not afford to guess wrong.

“Let me take both.”

“So tomorrow you can say you found them together?”

“I found them here.”

“Yes. Here.”

He pressed his fingers flatter. She could take the paper only by pulling against him. Even with two sound hands she would have lost something doing it.

“What do you need recorded?”

“That I released the South original to you. That you saw both seals. That you knew what the papers said when you took them.”

“Record it.”

He drew a sheet toward him. She watched him write. At the bottom he left room for her name.

Through the front window she could see people walking home. North lay beyond the Registry walls in that direction. Her brother would be there. South’s seed store lay the other way; its grain would not walk itself upstairs.

The clock had advanced six minutes.

“If I freeze the assignment,” she said, “North keeps protection.”

“Yes.”

“And South gets this.” She touched the edge of its certificate.

“South already has that.”

She read his receipt before she signed it. He had written exactly what she had asked him to record. Nothing about why he had done it.

He released the certificate. She put both originals inside her coat, one after the other, keeping their seals flat. Her inspection key pressed against her right thigh through its pocket.

“Enter the dispute,” she said. “Freeze the assignment.”

Ivo’s pen stayed above the sheet.

“Your license.”

“I know.”

She worked it out with her right hand. Without it, she could still reach the lock. What anyone there would let her do was another matter.

He cleared a space on the desk. Sera laid her license in it and took her hand away.

## P6

Ivo put two mugs on the desk. Sera looked at the second one. He had remembered how she took her tea. She wished he had forgotten.

Her left wrist was a white parcel of pain inside its splint. She kept it against her body; that hand could grip nothing. With her right, she took the North certificate from her coat and laid it on the desk.

“That isn’t the only one,” she said quietly.

Something shifted in Ivo’s face, too little for her to name. He opened the drawer, took out the South certificate, and placed it beside the first.

Paid for. All of it.

The two seals shone in the lamplight. Each confirmed registration. Neither promised there was room for the water.

Sera looked at him. He looked at the mugs. She could have asked again. Instead she left the question where it was, between the papers and the tea.

## P8

Two basins. One lock. Two certificates bearing the same authentic seal.

Licensed inspector Sera Venn knows what the Ninth Lock can do. At six o’clock, it can protect North Basin or South. The Registry has sold protection to both.

The second original is in the hands of Ivo Renn, her estranged former apprentice. He registered it legally. He has yet to tell her why.

Sera can freeze the disputed assignment by surrendering her license. North would keep its protection—and her brother his safety. South would face the tide with the city’s communal seed store in its path.

Before the warning bell, she needs the paper, the truth behind it, and a decision she can defend to the people left carrying the loss.
