# 10. A Moon with a Scar

This time, the moon arrived at the library with a small crowd walking beside it.

Mr. Moss pushed the trolley. Dad watched the box. Mila carried the notebook, and Noah carried nothing so he could open the doors.

Mrs. Bell was waiting at the worktable.

“Oh, my moon.”

“Our moon,” Mila said, then wondered if that sounded rude.

But Mrs. Bell smiled. “Quite right.”

They lifted the frame out and laid it on clean paper. Mrs. Bell examined the tear.

“The lid caught when I packed it this morning,” she said. “I thought I'd only bent the cardboard.”

She brought tissue paper and a little bowl of paste. Noah washed his hands, and Mila washed hers.

“We need a patch a bit bigger than the tear,” Mrs. Bell said. “Then we'll leave it flat until it's dry.”

Mila chose a clean white sheet. She wanted the mend to disappear.

Noah took one of Mila's paper stars from the drawer.

“What about this shape?”

He laid it beside the tear. The points spread wide enough to cover the split.

“Everyone would see it,” Mila said.

“They'll see a square patch too.”

“We could make a round one.”

Noah put the star down. “We could.”

Mila held the tissue against the light. Even one extra layer would show a little. She looked at the star again. It was the uneven one she had liked that morning.

“Let's try it.”

Noah traced around it gently. Mila cut the tissue while he held the sheet steady. The last point was tricky, so she turned the paper instead of twisting the scissors.

Mrs. Bell showed them how little paste they needed. Together they brushed it around the tear and smoothed the patch over it.

Mila lifted her hands slowly.

The star stayed where they had put it.

“Now we wait,” said Mrs. Bell.

Mila looked at the clock.

It was still afternoon. The walk wasn't until the sky grew dark. But waiting felt much longer than looking for something.

She wanted to touch the patch to see if it was dry.

Instead, she and Noah measured strings for the paper stars. His sums were still on the notebook's first page. They worked just as well as they had before the moon went missing.

At another table, Aunt June made new labels for the garden boxes. Mr. Moss crossed out the old messages with a thick pen.

“One job each,” he said, showing Mila a label that read EMPTY POTS.

She fetched the dinosaur from the windowsill and set it on top of the stack.

“What does his label say?” Noah asked.

“Guard.”

“He's not very big.”

“Neither are the pots.”

They finished the strings while the clock's hands crept around.

By the time the patch was dry, the yard was busy again. Lanterns lined the tables, waiting for their little battery lights.

Mrs. Bell checked the mend, then let Mila lift the frame with Noah.

The paper held.

Dad fixed two cloth loops to the frame and hung it from a smooth wooden bar. Mila and Noah tied their strings of stars along the bottom.

“Would you two like to help carry it?” he asked. “I'll hold the middle and take the weight.”

“Both of us?” Mila asked.

“It's a wide moon.”

Noah tried one end of the bar. Mila took the other. They practiced moving around a chair without knocking the stars against it.

They bumped the chair once.

On the next try, they slowed down together.

Behind the white paper, the star patch showed faintly. Mila could still see the crooked join she had pasted yesterday.

She checked the strings one last time.

Everything was ready.
