# Chapter Four: The Accommodation

They reached the counting house after three, carrying a drawing they had made twice and a question nobody had answered.

Tavin had cleared Ragna's desk. On it lay six packets of slips, each weighted with a clean stone. Beside them he had put a plate with half a loaf on it.

“Your mother sent that?” Ragna asked.

“I bought it. You may have some, though.”

She broke off a piece. The first stale mouthful made her ravenous.

Neri laid the drawing beside the plate. Tavin bent over it.

“Where was he standing?”

“We don't know,” Ragna said.

“And that was gone?”

“It was gone when we saw it. We don't know when it was removed.”

Neri took the bread Ragna offered her but did not eat it. “There was a notice about the tools. Brann may not be able to take his own out.”

“Brann?”

“The porter,” Ragna said. “His sister works there. They have rooms belonging to the works.”

Tavin straightened. “They can stay with us.”

“Have you asked your mother?”

“She won't mind.”

“Ask her.”

He opened his mouth, then thought better of it. Ragna wished she could do that more often.

Neri took out the signed repair bill. “Can you enter this? They can't say it wasn't finished.”

Ragna recorded it and gave her a receipt. Neri checked the amount. Before she left, she put the untouched bread in her sack.

“Orren will want it,” she said. “He has decided eating is his work.”

When she had gone, Tavin showed Ragna the packets. Each represented a household or a workshop. Where the withdrawal slips agreed with the wage books, he had made a provisional balance. Where they did not, he had written both sums in the margin.

“We can get signatures for these,” he said, pointing to three of the packets. “They kept their own tallies.”

“Signatures from whom?”

“The people they paid.”

“It would take days.”

“We have days. We have no ledger.”

She looked at the careful stones. He had marked the doubtful amounts with scraps of red thread rather than ink so that nobody could mistake them for charges.

“All right,” she said. “We begin with those. But write provisional at the top.”

“It's already there.”

Of course it was. She ate another piece of bread and began to read.

The door opened before she finished the first packet. The clerk nearest it stood so abruptly that Ragna thought Harrow had returned. Instead Merrok entered alone. Mud had dried on the edge of his coat, and there was a tear at the seam below one pocket. He noticed her noticing it and pulled the coat straight.

“Send the others home,” he said.

“We close at four.”

“It is ten minutes to four.”

Ragna set down the packet. “Tavin stays.”

Merrok looked at him.

“He heard the charge when I did. He has been reconstructing the balances. If you wish to discuss the accounts, he needs to hear it.”

“Does he speak for you now?”

“No. I do.”

Merrok took a chair and waited while the other clerks put away their work. Ragna could hear their coats being shaken out. Nobody said good night. When the door shut, the room seemed much larger.

“I hear you have been at Blackwater,” Merrok said.

“I went with a fitter. There is a disputed delivery.”

“There is an inquiry.”

“Whose?”

“The Ministry's.”

“And the Ministry has taken the only book that will show what our people are owed.”

He rubbed a thumb across the seam of his pocket. “Which is why I am here.”

From inside his coat he took a leather case and two folded sheets. He placed the case on the desk. It made a soft, substantial sound.

Ragna did not touch it.

“I have arranged an accommodation,” he said. “For the households affected by the suspension. A payment now, to be taken into account when the balances are settled.”

“From which fund?”

“The source need not concern them.”

“It concerns me.”

“Then call it my undertaking.”

Tavin leaned forward. Ragna could almost feel his desire to open the case.

“How much?” she asked.

Merrok gave her a schedule. It allowed a payment equal to roughly a week's ordinary earnings for each registered worker, with a smaller amount for dependants. For some households it was more than they needed immediately. For others it would cover food and nothing else. It was the first useful sum anyone had put on her desk since the seizure.

She read the second sheet.

The heading called it a receipt. Under the spaces for names and amounts, the household would acknowledge lawful custody of the records and accept the bank's determination of any disputed charges. It also released claims arising from the removal of materials under the current inquiry.

“What materials?” she asked.

“Those under examination.”

“Does this include the twenty bars Sella's workshop never received?”

Merrok looked up sharply.

“I cannot answer a question about an unverified assertion.”

“Then take that sentence out.”

“The form has been agreed.”

“By the households?”

“By the people providing the money.”

Ragna ran a finger under the release. “This says they accept a determination that hasn't been made. What is the payment for?”

“To keep them fed.”

“Then why does it speak about materials?”

“Because no one will provide relief indefinitely while claims remain without limit.”

Tavin made a sound. Merrok turned to him.

“You have something to contribute?”

“He isn't asking you to provide it indefinitely,” Tavin said. “He isn't asking you anything. He's dead.”

Ragna closed her eyes for a moment.

Merrok put the schedule flat on the desk. “I have twenty-seven households in difficulty, a Ministry order that I cannot dismiss by wishing it away, and three partners who will not advance reserve funds against balances they cannot verify. If you can find an arrangement acceptable to all of them by morning, I will gladly withdraw mine.”

“Three partners?” Ragna asked.

“The reserve requires two signatures. Neither of the two I approached would put a name to it without the underlying record.”

“Did you show them the wage books?”

“I showed them what I had.”

She looked at the packets beneath their stones. He saw where she was looking and said nothing.

“Leave the form,” she said.

“And the money?”

“Take it with you.”

Tavin turned towards her, furious.

“I haven't refused it,” she said. “It is not mine to refuse. The people whose names go on this should read it.”

Merrok sat back. The tightness at his mouth eased.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “In the old weigh house. I will explain the terms myself.”

“At two. Sella is burying her husband in the morning.”

He looked down at his hands.

“Yes,” he said. “At two.”

He took the case and left both sheets. At the door he paused.

“You have worked here for eleven years, Ragna. I know what that has cost you. Be careful whose anger you borrow.”

After the door closed, Tavin walked to it and slid the bolt.

“You could have kept it until they decided.”

“It came with a form.”

“We could have put the form in a drawer.”

“And when somebody was hungry tonight?”

He said nothing.

She pushed the schedule towards him. “Copy it. All of it, including the part that looks dull.”

“Is there another kind?”

“The dull part is where he put the claims.”

Tavin sat. His pen moved hard enough to scratch. Ragna began a second copy beside him.

Sella was at the workshop, wrapping the finished school hinges in sacking. Brann stood by the bench with a folded coat under his arm. His sister had come with him, still wearing the leather gloves Ragna had seen at the chain shed.

Ragna stopped in the doorway. Brann saw the copied form in her hand and stepped away from the bench.

“It is an offer,” she said. “Not an order.”

His sister took it from her. She read the first paragraph and gave it back.

“They've put the gatekeeper's son on the night watch,” she said. “He's never worked a winch in his life. They told us we may take a day away if we put it in writing.”

“Did you?”

“No. I came home at the end of my shift.”

She flexed her hands inside the gloves. The left one did not open fully.

“Tavin's mother has room,” Ragna said. “He was going to ask her. You would need to speak to her yourselves.”

Brann shook his head. “We have a room.”

“At the works,” Neri said.

“Yes. At the works. With our clothes and our mother's plates in it.”

“Then fetch them.”

“Her plates fill a chest.”

Neri looked at the little handcart under the bench. Sella followed her eyes, then looked at Ragna.

“You can take it,” she told Brann. “It has to be back before the school collection tomorrow.”

Brann put down the coat. “They'll see us.”

“Then they will know you took your plates,” his sister said. “I'm not leaving them to the gatekeeper's boy.”

They argued quietly over what could fit on the cart. A blanket, the chest, two bundles of clothes. Brann wanted to leave the chest and carry the plates wrapped in shirts. His sister said she had wrapped them once to bring them here from their mother's house and would not do it again while a cart was available.

Ragna left them arguing and went back to the counting room. Tavin was still there, finishing the schedule. She told him what his mother was being asked to consider.

He washed his pen at once. “I'll take them to her.”

“You don't have to move them tonight.”

“No,” he said. “They do.”

Outside the workshop, he took one handle of the cart. Neri took the other. Brann's sister told them to leave room by the wheel for a sack of coal she had paid for, and Ragna found herself holding a lamp while four people settled how to move a life in one journey.

By the time she reached her own room, the lamps in the passage had dimmed. A neighbour's door stood open, letting out the smell of fried onions. Ragna had meant to buy some two nights earlier and had decided they could wait. Now she found herself standing still in the passage, remembering Dorrik arguing over them, refusing to pay for the green shoots as if they were part of the bulb.

Her room held a bed, a table and the narrow shelves she had paid a carpenter to fit around a crooked chimney breast. The carpenter had been a witch with a squint and a tendency to sing when she worked. Ragna had kept her bill for seven years because the shelves were the best thing in the room.

She put her bag on the table and lit the stove. There was soup enough for one person and bread enough for none. She ate the soup from the pan, washed it, and sat down to write out the names of the reserve partners.

She knew two of them professionally. One had once returned an account because a clerk had abbreviated a workshop's name. The other had approved a furnace repair after three men had suffered burns, then asked why nobody had applied before anyone was hurt.

Ragna had not applied. The foreman had told her repairs belonged in next year's estimate. She had accepted that answer and entered the burns under relief.

She put down her pen.

There was a knock. Before she could rise, Tavin called through the door.

“They're in. My mother says if either of them snores, they can negotiate.”

Ragna opened the door. He held a covered bowl.

“She also said you won't have eaten.”

“I have.”

“She said you'd say that.”

He put the bowl on the table. Inside were potatoes, cabbage and a piece of smoked fish. Ragna had not realised he knew where she lived.

“Thank her,” she said.

“She made them agree to share the fuel before she helped unload. She won't have strangers moved into her house as if they were furniture.”

“Quite right.”

He stayed standing by the table. “Could we pay something ourselves?”

“How much have you got?”

“Not just me. People.”

“Some people are waiting for their wages.”

“Some aren't.”

Ragna took her purse out of the bag. There were coins for rent, coal, food and an amount she had been saving to replace the lenses in her reading frame. She sorted them into four small piles. Tavin watched but did not offer an opinion.

“This won't cover a workshop,” she said.

“It might cover a person.”

“And next week?”

He looked at the wall, then back at her. “We work on next week.”

She pushed two of the piles together. A few meals, perhaps. She left the other two piles where they were.

“We need rules,” she said. “People must know whether it's a gift or a debt. They must know who decides.”

“Then write them.”

“No. I'll bring paper tomorrow. They can tell us what to write.”

Tavin looked at the coins for a long time. “I'll ask my mother.”

When he had gone, Ragna uncovered the bowl again. The fish was nearly all bone. She ate slowly, picking each one out with her fingers, and wrote the three partner names on a fresh sheet before she let herself go to bed.
