## F1-D

The ticket room smelled of lamp oil and chalk ground off dead stubs. Neri kept her right hand in her coat, thumb on the token's scalloped edge — stamped brass no bigger than a thumbnail, eight years of other people's dinners.

Olin stood where the lamp didn't reach, making a door of himself. Good coat, buttons done wrong, and the smell of that yellow soap from basins where you scrubbed fast and left faster.

"You could tell me who," Neri said. "I'd still say good morning to you after."

"I told you. Someone's waiting."

"Someone." She glanced at the clock in its brass cage. "They'll stamp that on the ticket. Fare for someone."

His jaw worked the way it used to when he was choosing between laughing and leaving. He did neither. He fed the clock another look, and a man at leisure does not feed a clock that many looks.

"The ferry waits," he said. "They hold her when it matters."

Eight minutes' walk, twelve across, gate shut at five to six. She had counted it all winter over other people's glasses. The ferry had never once waited for anybody's tragedy.

"Twenty-six past," she said. "You've spent six minutes making a stranger of yourself. Slow work, for you."

"Give it over, Neri."

She should hate him cleanly; it would cost less. It was the buttons that stopped her — Olin, who set hooks with his eyes shut, dressed like a man summoned. Somebody had called him out of a warm room in a hurry, and he could not say the name without owing her all of it, and Olin had never once paid a debt he could duck.

She drew the token out of her right pocket. It had her hand's heat in it. She pressed it into his palm and folded his fingers over it herself, as you'd close a dead man's hand on his fare.

"Gate's five to the hour," she said. "Walk like a brother and you'll make it."

## F2-D

At noon the booth clock and the hallway clock disagreed by a minute, and Rafi believed the booth. He had the room till 12:20, a file due at 12:30, and one cable that worked provided nobody respected it. Then Leena filled his doorway, Pia beside her with the violin case, the pair arranged like weather coming in.

"Fifteen minutes," Leena said.

"Funny," Rafi said. "I was just asking the universe for the same thing."

Pia, behind her mother's shoulder, waved small with the hand not holding the rosin, then appeared to regret owning a body. Twelve, maybe. Old enough to know she was being used as a reason; too polite to invoice anybody.

"We drove forty minutes," Leena said. She did not come in. Once she'd have been inside rewiring his gain; today her boots stayed on the hallway carpet like it was a border.

"Both files are due at half past. My slot ends at 12:20. The man after me plays the accordion, which God will punish, but the booking is the booking."

"So one of us wastes," Leena said. "Since you know the man. It ain't charity when it's family."

Pia studied the ceiling. "There's a bus at twelve-forty, Mum."

Rafi had been useful to people all his life, the way a ladder is useful, and what he wanted — stupidly, at noon on a Tuesday — was for Leena to ask him and mean him. She stood there not asking, sparing Pia the argument by having it louder.

The booth clock said 12:05. He took the key off the desk, held it a second so the brass could cool its opinion of him, and laid it flat in Pia's palm.

"Stand off the left wall; it eats high strings. You tune?"

"I tune," Pia said, with dignity.

He opened the booth door and she went in, and at the glass Leena put one hand flat, not waving. Rafi pressed record. The red light came on, and the rest of his noon belonged to somebody else.
