# Independent full-manuscript reader report

**Book:** *Mila and the Missing Moon*  
**Review role:** reader advocate  
**Assessment:** The complete twelve-chapter manuscript delivers the brief's gentle, fair detective story for an eight-year-old listening with a parent. I found **no concrete revision required** on this reader pass. The one clarity opportunity below is discretionary, not a continuity error. This is a reader-experience assessment, not a claim of publishability.

## Reading experience

The opening absence is immediately understandable: Mila has made stars for a large moon that is gone. The physical evidence gives a listener things to picture and remember: fuzzy yellow wool, pale wheel tracks, a wheel with a missing bite, and a box with two messages. Noah's ruler and drawing make him a real investigative partner. Mila's premature accusation has a cost Bea is allowed to feel; the apology in chapter 3 and Mila's invitation to Bea in chapter 11 provide separate, credible steps toward repair. The star patch turns a mishap into a visible part of the lantern, and the walk fulfills the book's promise before the quiet bedtime close.

The prose generally reads aloud cleanly: short paragraphs, distinct conversational turns, concrete sensory detail, and humor that does not depend on an illustration. Chapter endings give natural pauses without repeatedly manufacturing peril. The solution does not require an unseen villain or a clue first supplied at the reveal. In particular, the reused-label rule appears with the STARS box in chapter 7, Mrs. Bell's MOON label is disclosed in chapter 8, and both sides are checked in chapter 9. Mr. Moss's account of moving a flat box, the trolley test, and the garden route support the causal chain. Adults provide access, safety, and help; Mila and Noah make the decisive deductions. I found no definite continuity or speaker-attribution error that changes comprehension, and no concrete stereotyping mechanism in the reviewed text.

## Prioritized finding

1. **Optional read-aloud orientation at the route test; audience-accessibility judgment, not an error.** Chapter 6, lines 41–43: “They headed toward the gate. The other path, past the bakery, lay beyond the table's old position. ‘If the trolley went right, wouldn't it have hit the table?’” The book has established the yard, but a child hearing the scene cannot see Noah's drawing. Holding the two paths, vanished table, and wheel curve in mind may briefly slow comprehension just as the pair proves which route to search. The reenactment in lines 63–79 already does much of the work. **Smallest intervention, if a human read-aloud exposes confusion:** add one concrete orientation beat from Mila's position before her question, placing the old table between the trolley and the bakery turn. Preserve the hands-on test and Noah's contribution; an explanatory paragraph would cost more than it gains.
