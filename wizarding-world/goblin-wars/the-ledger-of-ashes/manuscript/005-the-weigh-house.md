# Chapter Five: The Weigh House

Sella had asked for no singing.

Some of the mourners clasped their hands; others held their hats or stared at the ground. A few were still wearing work aprons. One woman had come from the furnaces without washing, and sweat had made little white channels through the dust on her face.

Ragna stood at the back. She had brought flowers from a stall and felt foolish holding them. The stallholder had asked what they were for, then charged her less. She had accepted the kindness and hated the question.

Sella spoke for less than a minute. She said Dorrik had been difficult about getting out of bed and careless with good clothes. He had remembered what people drank. He had once spent six days fitting a gate because the man who ordered it had a bad hand and could not work an ordinary catch.

“He wasn't paid for the extra days,” she said. “I was angry about that at the time.”

She stopped. Neri took her hand, but Sella shook her head and let it go. Orren stood between them with his coat buttoned wrong. He had brought the little wooden boat. He held it throughout the burial and took it home again.

When the earth had been put back, Ragna set her flowers at the edge with the others. Neri was speaking to two of the fitters. Sella had already begun thanking people for coming. She accepted a loaf from the woman in the furnace apron and put it in her basket as if it were something she had bought.

At the path, Brann waited for Ragna. His sister stood a little way off, talking to Tavin's mother. They had not gone back to work that morning.

“Vessa told me to take a day,” he said. “She says if they want a notice about it, they may come and ask her.”

“She's your older sister?”

“By thirteen minutes. She counts them whenever necessary.”

He looked towards the fresh earth.

“The report says he went down alone. I didn't know what they put in it until last night.”

“Who showed it to you?”

“Pell read it. He asked if I would confirm it.”

“Did you?”

“No.” He scraped mud from his boot with the edge of a stone. “I told him I was at the bottom. I couldn't say how Dorrik got there.”

“That is what you should say.”

“It's what I've said.”

Ragna stepped aside to let him join his sister.

The old weigh house had not weighed anything in eleven years. Its beam still hung above the central table, one pan lower than the other, though both were empty. Children had discovered that a whisper into the left pan came out of the right one twice as loud. When Ragna arrived, Orren was testing whether it would work for a whistle.

Sella told him once to stop. He stopped.

The room filled slowly. People came in pairs from workshops, or alone with someone else's name folded in a pocket. The woman with the broken lantern had brought it again; Ragna recognised her now as Edda, who worked on the small furnace. She needed a new wick collar and refused to throw away a lantern her aunt had made.

Fenn, a glassworker whose fingers were thickened by old burns, arrived carrying two stools. He had been told there might not be enough seats and took such predictions personally.

Merrok came at two exactly, with a bank clerk and the leather case. He put the case beneath the hanging pan.

“There is no need for speeches,” he said.

“Then you won't need long,” Sella said.

Ragna sat beside Tavin, who had copied the schedule six times. They passed the sheets along the benches. She watched people read, mouth a word, turn a page back. A man near the door held his copy upside down until Edda moved beside him and read it quietly to him.

Merrok explained the payments. A week's ordinary earnings, adjusted for the registered household. No interest. Any amount eventually proved due would be credited, less the payment already received.

“And if the bank finds nothing due?” Fenn asked.

“The payment will not be recovered.”

Several people looked up.

“Put that on the paper,” Sella said.

The bank clerk began to object. Merrok held up a hand. After a moment he nodded, and the clerk made the addition on the receipt form. He read it aloud. Tavin copied the words.

“Can we take the money today?” asked a fitter who had left Sella's workshop to find work with his cousin.

“Yes,” Merrok said.

“And go back tomorrow if more of our wages are due?”

“Your household's account will be settled through the bank.”

The fitter, Brelk, rubbed the back of his neck. “Does that mean yes?”

Ragna pointed to the release clause on her copy. “It means you accept that determination. The form doesn't give you another way to challenge it.”

Merrok looked at her. “If the charges are correct, what would you challenge?”

“If they're wrong?” Brelk asked.

“We will correct them.”

“Who is we?”

“The bank,” Merrok said.

Brelk looked at Sella, who said nothing. His boots were wet and poorly repaired. He had taken two days' extra work in another shop. What could she tell him the right to challenge a charge was worth?

“What happens if I don't sign?” he asked.

“The inquiry continues,” Merrok said. “And your payment waits on the balances.”

Edda put down her sheet. “That sounds like paying us to stop asking.”

“It sounds like a week's food,” said the woman beside her.

“So does a week's wages.”

“Can you put those on the table?”

Edda looked away. The woman picked up the form again.

Sella raised her voice over them. “Nobody is deciding for anyone else's household. If you want the payment, read what you are giving for it. If you want help reading, ask. Don't put your name down because somebody has looked at you.”

“Does that include you?” Merrok asked.

“Yes.”

He waited as if there ought to have been more. Sella folded her hands.

Brelk was first to the table. He asked the clerk to read the form, including the addition. Then he signed. The case opened with two clicks, and the clerk counted money into his hand.

Brelk put it in a pouch. “My son has a cough that keeps him awake. The room by the stove costs more. That's what I am buying.”

Nobody had asked him to explain. Ragna wished she could tell him that without making it another public judgement.

He went back to his place. Two other people rose, then a third. The clerk found a rhythm: name, amount, form, signature. Each receipt went into a flat box beside the case. Ragna watched the clerk stack the receipts, their edges square.

When the last of the four sat down, Merrok closed the case.

“The offer remains open until tomorrow evening. The bank clerk will attend here between noon and two.”

“Not at the counting house?” Ragna asked.

“It is better not to confuse the arrangements.”

She heard the warning in that. The money had come with its own office.

Merrok gathered the unsigned forms. Sella asked for one and he left it with her. At the door he turned back.

“I know some of you think a refusal will improve your position. It may. It will not improve your position tonight.”

He went out before anyone answered.

For a while the only sound was Orren turning the little boat over in his hands. Then Fenn picked up one of his stools and set it in front of the table.

“Now,” he said, “what about tonight?”

Tavin stood so quickly that he caught his knee on the bench. He put a folded cloth on the table and opened it. Coins lay inside, mixed with a button he had mistaken for a knut.

“Some of us thought we could put something together.”

“Who is some of us?” Edda asked.

“Me. My mother. Ragna.”

Ragna put her purse beside the cloth. She had left the rent money at home. She told them what she was offering, and that it was a gift.

“It won't pay everybody,” she said. “I don't want anyone to think it will.”

“It won't pay this room,” Fenn said. “But I have flour. It was delivered before the wages stopped.”

“Flour isn't free just because it's been delivered,” the moulding-sand supplier said from the door.

Her name was Hirk. She had come in late and stood holding her cap against her stomach.

“No,” Fenn said. “Mine is paid for.”

“I can let Sella wait until Friday. I cannot let every workshop wait until Friday. I owe the man who brings the sand, and his mule eats whether we have wages or not.”

Sella nodded. “I told you I understood.”

“I'm telling them.”

Ragna picked up her pen. “We need to know what we have, and what people need first.”

“Who sees the needs?” Vessa asked.

“Who do you want?”

“Not everybody in this room.”

They argued. A man wanted each household given the same amount. Edda said a single worker and a household of six could not eat the same money. Fenn offered to divide by children, then was reminded that several old people lived with their families and did not become cheaper because they were older. Someone proposed loans, recoverable when the bank paid. Brelk asked what would happen if the bank never paid.

At that, the room became quiet again.

“Then a loan is a bill for being hungry,” Vessa said.

“Gifts run out,” Hirk answered.

“So do loans. They just leave something behind.”

They settled on food for the next two days, and fuel where there was none. No promise beyond that. Anyone could give food or money; anyone could ask for food or fuel. Three people would hold the list of needs and decide together: Sella, Fenn and Vessa. Ragna would keep the receipts and count the fund in front of them. If they disagreed, they would tell the next meeting rather than settle it by lending secretly.

“It ought to have a name,” Tavin said.

“It has flour,” Fenn said. “Start with that.”

Brelk came to the table while Ragna was adding the coins. He put down two sickles.

“You don't have to,” she said.

“I know what I have to do. Put it with the rest.”

She did. He went to help Fenn carry the flour.

By four, seven households had an arrangement for the evening. Others had agreed to share meals or wait until tomorrow. Ragna could not check all those promises. She watched a woman who had arrived with an empty coal bag leave with it heavy against her leg.

Sella asked Neri to take Orren home. When they had gone, she put the unsigned receipt in front of Ragna.

“I want a way to ask for our balance without signing this.”

“A petition,” Fenn said.

“To whom?”

“The bank partners. And the Ministry for the inquiry.”

“Not one petition,” Ragna said. “They'll each say it's the other's business.”

“Then two.”

She drew two headings. To the bank: verified payment against wages and access to a certified copy of the relevant account. To the Ministry: preservation of the delivery records and a hearing on the circumstances of Dorrik's death. Sella made her add the right to bring someone to read any paper a family was asked to sign.

Brann stood at the edge of the table.

“I didn't see the top of the shaft,” he said.

“You won't be asked to say you did,” Sella replied.

He stayed while they wrote. He did not sign until she read his statement aloud: he had been below when the lift came down at an angle; he had found Dorrik there. Nothing about a push, nothing about who gave an order. He crossed out one word himself and put his name beneath the correction.

Tavin asked how many copies were needed.

“One for each recipient,” Ragna said. “One for us.”

“And everyone who came?”

“Let us see who agrees to have their names copied first.”

He put down the blank sheets. “It's our claim.”

“It is. Every person's name on it is their own.”

Brelk returned with flour on his coat and read the petition over Fenn's shoulder. “Can I sign, even after taking his money?”

Ragna turned the receipt until she could see the exact words again.

“The Ministry petition asks about the death,” she said. “The materials release may affect the bank claim. I can't promise what they'll say about it.”

He considered that, then signed the Ministry petition and left the bank claim untouched.

When the room had emptied, the weighing pan still hung lower on the left. Orren had left a single rivet inside it. Ragna took it out and laid it by the door, where he would see it next time.
