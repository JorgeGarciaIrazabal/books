# Chapter 7: The Midnight Tax Storm

Finding the right house in Spain felt wonderful. Then a freezing January storm rattled the New Jersey windows at eleven o'clock at night and woke up every grown-up worry in the house.

Dining chairs lay buried under IRS booklets, Spanish tax treaty sheets, European health insurance charts, and currency tables. Rain lashed the porch glass while thunder rumbled across the roof.

"Look at all this," Jorge muttered, rubbing his tired eyes as twelve browser tabs glared on his laptop screen. "What if American and Spanish tax rules tangle us in knots? What if the stock market drops twenty percent right after I quit, right before we finish selling this house?"

Worst of all, Garbanzo had been watching Jorge panic over the keyboard for two straight hours. Suddenly the mini bot's tiny cooling fan shrieked like a teakettle, and his Double-Counting Bug snapped completely haywire.

*BEEP-BEEP-BEEP! DING!*

Garbanzo's chest panel blinked bright orange and neon green like an ambulance. He hopped up on all four stubby metal feet beside the sugar bowl and threw both tiny arms skyward.

"Problem solved!" Garbanzo screamed over the rain. Pink confetti shot straight across Marissa's open insurance folder. "I counted the New Jersey house twice before you even sold it! I mixed your old-age savings with today's grocery cash and multiplied a bank transfer by four! You have endless treasure! Quit tomorrow! Buy a yacht made of chocolate ice cream!"

"Stop yelling about ice-cream boats!" Marissa laughed nervously while batting pink confetti out of her hair. "Jorge, turn him off! If he mixes locked savings with spendable cash one more time, I'm going to lose my mind!"

Thunder cracked overhead. Jorge wasn't letting fear or buggy math push them backward.

"Hold the flashlight, Marissa," Jorge said sharply, popping open his kitchen toolbox.

Grabbing a miniature screwdriver and a silver USB cord, Jorge gently caught his hopping little bot by both elbows.

"Hey! Gentle with my shell!" Garbanzo squeaked as Jorge carefully unscrewed the small square panel on his back. "I am delicate artistic genius!"

"You are a walking bug factory," Jorge murmured, plugging his laptop cord straight into Garbanzo's tiny board. Confetti dust floated onto the keyboard as lines of messy code scrolled down the dark screen.

Rain drummed furiously against the dark window. Squinting hard through tired eyes, Jorge hunted line by line until he spotted the sneaky mistake he had written months ago on a rushed Sunday night.

"Aha!" Jorge pointed. "No double paycheck estimates. Zero out pocket-to-pocket transfers so moving cash doesn't invent fake money. Keep locked future retirement money strictly separated from cash we spend right now."

His thumb hit `ENTER`.

*ZAP!*

One bright spark snapped cleanly off Garbanzo's copper chip. The little robot sneezed out three lonely pieces of leftover blue paper, blinked clear steady eyes, and lowered both stubby arms cleanly to his sides.

"Update finished," Garbanzo reported in a calm, polite voice. "Internal transfers zeroed. Safe spending checked. Real savings looking steady."

Marissa let out a long breath and pulled over two crisp notebooks stamped cleanly with Saturday's **Cash Cushion** number. "All right. Even if stock prices catch a nasty winter cold right after we quit, our side savings cover groceries, home taxes, utilities, and school without touching emergency stocks."

"Mostly," Jorge sighed, leaning back in his chair with a frown. "Unless a really crummy market year hits right when we want to take a big expensive Christmas trip."

Creak.

The stairs door swung gently open. Six-year-old Clara shuffled into the dining room rubbing one sleepy eye, dressed in red flannel pajamas and her lucky purple unicorn socks.

"Too loud," she yawned, climbing up onto the chair beside Marissa to inspect the scary piles of folders. "Why are you still fighting scary math papers?"

"Not fighting, kiddo," Marissa smiled softly, kissing her warm forehead. "Just trying to make sure if stock money shrinks for a stormy year or two, we don't have to give up good food or run back to Manhattan."

Clara blinked once at the paper vacation calendar tacked to the wall, then tilted her head like it was the simplest puzzle in kindergarten.

"Well, duh," Clara said matter-of-factly. "If a year gets all stormy and grumpy outside, we just skip the fancy winter plane trip that year. We stay home, make cozy blanket forts, and do popcorn movie night until sunny numbers come back."

Silence fell over the dining room, broken only by soft rain tapping the porch roof.

Jorge stared at his six-year-old daughter, then burst into a wide, relieved laugh. "Wait. Did our six-year-old just solve our whole rainy-day budget?"

Marissa smiled wide, pulled Clara onto her lap, and squeezed her tight.

Then Jorge stood up and gathered the scary two-country tax printouts, residency checklists, and health forms into one brown folder. Across the front he wrote in thick black marker:

`CHECK WITH REAL TAX AND LEGAL ADVISORS FIRST.`

"Time to call real experts," Jorge told Marissa, setting the folder squarely beside his briefcase. "Tomorrow morning I set up meetings with professionals in both countries."

Over on the kitchen counter, warm golden light spilled peacefully around the copper pot lid, lighting the wet dark room like a steady little sunrise.
