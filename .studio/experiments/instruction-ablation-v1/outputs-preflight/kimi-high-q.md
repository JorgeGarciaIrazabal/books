## F1-D

The ticket room smelled of coal dust and wet wool. The clerk had gone to supper and left the lantern lit, which Neri chose to read as trust rather than arithmetic. Twenty past five. The board said: last ferry, 17:40, chalked in a hand that did not expect argument.

The token sat in her right coat pocket. She touched it through the cloth, the way she had every morning for four years. Stamped brass: one person, one crossing, spent at the plank. Her thumb had worn one edge smooth. It was the only warm thing she owned that still worked.

Olin stood with his back to the stove, drying his cuffs, a man waiting for weather to break.

"I have to see someone," he said.

"How urgent," Neri said, "and how vague. My someones usually come with names."

"It's not—" Olin stopped. "It's not something I can explain."

"Something," she said. "Someone." She looked at the clock instead of him; safer both ways. "Eight minutes to the landing. Twenty to the ferry. And you want my token."

"I want," he said carefully, "not to ask you for it."

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that smelled of low tide.

Neri kept her palm flat over her right pocket, casual as a hand at rest. Four years of deck work, rope-burn to the shoulder, winters of mending other people's nets — all of it folded into one ounce of brass, and here stood blood, asking her to unfold.

"Walk with me," she said. "You have until the plank to give me the name. A true name buys the token."

"And if I don't?"

"Then watch the sternlight and think of me fondly," she said. "You always were good at farewells."

He looked at her — the first honest look all evening — and she watched what it cost him, and let him keep the change. He pulled the door wide. Rain leaned in to see.

"Seventeen twenty-eight," she told the clerk's empty chair, settling her hand over the brass. "Right pocket," she said, to herself mostly, and followed her brother out.

## F2-D

At noon the booth fan sounded like one tired cicada. Rafi had twenty minutes of key time, sides marked up until the pencil gave out, and a twelve-thirty deadline that had eaten his week.

Leena arrived with Pia and without apology. The violin, at least, wore its case.

"Don't get up," Leena said, as though he'd been offered a choice. "Pia's thing is due at twelve-thirty. Fifteen minutes, clean. You know how portals are."

"Portals." Pia, deep in a census of the hallway tiles, winced at the word. He liked her for it.

"You're wondering why I didn't call first," Leena said, to a point somewhere south of his chin. "Pia. Tell your uncle the situation."

"Mom." A whole childhood of summers in one syllable. "We talked about this."

There it was: the errand assembled out of spare parts, Pia issued as postage. Rafi felt the old machinery engage. Rafi has the room. Rafi will fix it. He was useful to this family on a schedule, like a bus route, and about as thanked.

The key sat on the desk, doing its impression of Switzerland.

He looked at his sides — six pages, one unpaid week — and at Leena's wrist, tapped out in apology-free arithmetic, and then at Pia, whose bow hand had gone white around the case handle, a girl doing advanced mathematics of her own: how to want something without becoming the argument.

"Pia." He waited until she looked at him. Her. Only her. "Do you want the booth?"

Her thumb moved on the latch. Today-trained in not saying yes. She nodded once — curt, professional, session-player grade.

"Twelve-oh-five," he said, and crossed the three steps, and put the key into her hand himself, folding her fingers over it so there could be no committee about custody. "Fifteen minutes. Red light means the rest of us are furniture."

"It ain't a favor if you keep an account," Leena said, as if speed could launder it.

Rafi picked up his dead folder and held the door wide.

"Then it's an account," he said. "Just the room. Not forgiveness. Go on in, maestro."
