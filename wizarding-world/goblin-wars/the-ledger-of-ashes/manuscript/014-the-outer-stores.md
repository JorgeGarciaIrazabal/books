# Chapter Fourteen: The Outer Stores

Neri counted her tools at the gate of Thorn.

She laid them on the board the guard provided: two hammers, three small files, a rule, pliers, a punch, spare pins and a folding knife. He wrote each down. When she pointed out that he had counted the pliers as two things, he told her to put everything away.

Ragna gave him the collection authority. The guard checked their names against the amendments on his own sheet. Mere had sent confirmation with the morning carrier. Without it, he said, they would have been turned back.

The supplier's attested invoice was at Sella's workshop, not in Ragna's bag. Ilva's statement and the advance copies were there as well. Ragna carried only the authority, a few blank sheets and food enough to avoid having to ask the depot for it.

“What work do you do?” the guard asked.

“Accounts.”

“Bank?”

“I am here to help collect a cart.”

He looked at her for another moment, then wrote clerk beside her name.

Thorn had been a mill before the Ministry took its lease. The wheel was gone, but the cut that had fed it still ran beside the lower wall. A brick office stood to the right of the main gate. Beyond it, the yard sloped down to a long vehicle shed and a pair of storehouses. A covered loading lane ran between the buildings towards a lower gate at the river. Ragna could see the towpath through its bars.

Two ways out, with open ground between them. Since the weigh house, Ragna looked for doors before chairs.

A porter led them to the vehicle shed. He wore a Ministry badge over a patched jersey and carried a ring of keys that struck his thigh as he walked.

“Mr Dole wants the receipt signed before you move it.”

“What receipt?” Neri asked.

“Vehicle returned as held.”

“Let me see the vehicle.”

He stopped, looked back and said, “That's where we're going.”

“Then good.”

He gave her a puzzled glance, then led them on.

The narrow cart stood beneath the shed's open side. Its shafts rested on a block. One wheel had been chocked with a piece of stone, and the brake lever was tied back with wire. Mere's initials had been painted on the rear crosspiece in fading white.

Neri put down her sack and went to the wheel. She crouched, felt the bracket and worked the lever carefully. It moved halfway and stopped.

“Somebody forced it with the brake on.”

The porter scratched his chin. “They said it came in like that.”

“It couldn't have come down this slope like that.”

“I wasn't here when it came.”

“Then you needn't sign that it did.”

She took out her knife and cut the wire. The lever dropped. The bracket remained crooked against the wheel rim.

Ragna stood at the back of the cart. Across the yard a storehouse door was open. Inside, several bundles of iron rested on low racks. One bundle had been unstrapped. The ends of the bars were turned towards the light.

She could see an M in a circle on the nearest one. She could not make out the batch number.

“Don't wander,” the porter said.

“I haven't.”

He followed her gaze to the bars, then went to fetch Mr Dole.

Neri tapped the bracket with her small hammer, listened, and put the hammer down.

“The pin is bent as well. I can do it. I need a little heat for the bracket, not enough to spoil it.”

“Will they allow it?”

“They asked for a fitter. Now they'll find out what one does.”

Mr Dole arrived carrying the receipt book. He was a wizard with a thick neck, ink on his smallest finger and a manner that suggested every minute spent away from the office would have to be made up later.

Neri showed him the brake. He did not pretend to understand it. He asked what she needed and how long she meant to take.

“An hour if nothing else has been damaged.”

“What else could be?”

“I'll tell you if I find it.”

He looked at the receipt. “You sign that the vehicle has been returned.”

“When it moves.”

“And the condition?”

“I'll write the condition I see. I won't write when it happened.”

Ragna expected an argument. Dole shrugged.

“Fine. Write it in the margin. I'm not putting a paragraph in the heading.”

He sent the porter for a brazier and a worker to tend it. Then he held the receipt book for Ragna to see.

The cart was listed against a consignment reference. It was the forty-bar order. In the column beside it, cargo received was marked complete.

“May I copy the reference?”

“You've brought a copy of it.”

“The reference on your return book.”

He sighed and turned the book so she could read. She copied the date and the whole string of numbers, including the final letter.

“Who received the cargo?”

“That is not the vehicle return.”

“I am authorised by the bank to obtain the supplier's record of the purchase.”

“Then go to the supplier.”

“We have. We need the depot receipt.”

Dole closed the book. “You have authority to collect a cart. If you want something else, write to the office.”

She had already written. She did not tell him again. Across the yard the storehouse worker lifted a cloth over the open bundle of bars. The M disappeared beneath it.

“Can we have the copy of this return receipt when we leave?” she asked.

“Yes. When you leave.”

The worker brought a small brazier on a trolley. He was a goblin, heavy through the shoulders, with grey hair cropped close to his scalp. He pushed the trolley slowly because one wheel dragged. The porter told him where to put it and went back towards the gate.

Neri had the brake pin in her hand. She looked up at the worker and stopped moving.

“Kelm?”

The man lowered his head. “Keep on with it.”

“What are you doing here?”

“I said keep on.”

Ragna went to the cart's other side. From there she could see Dole return to the office. The guard at the main gate was examining another delivery. The porter had stopped to talk to a driver by the river lane.

“Kelm signed the Blackwater receipt,” Neri said quietly.

The man put his hands over the brazier, though it was not yet hot. The skin across one knuckle had split.

“I signed a receipt for forty bars at Blackwater. That's all it said.”

“We know,” Ragna replied.

“No, you don't.”

His voice rose enough that all three looked towards the office. Nobody came out.

Neri turned the bent pin in her pliers. “My father came to ask about the other load.”

Kelm stared into the unlit brazier.

“I know who your father was.”

“I know you do.”

Ragna took the kindling from the trolley and began laying it in the brazier. She needed something to do that did not look like standing over him. Neri had brought a little packet of fire chips from the workshop. She set one under the wood and touched it until a low flame took hold.

For a while the only sound was Kelm breathing and the first crackle of the fire.

“Are you employed here?” Ragna asked.

“No.”

“Can you leave?”

He looked at her then. His eyes were bloodshot, and there was a line of dirt in the crease below his lower lip.

“They say when the loading account is settled. They say I'm assisting the inquiry. They have my works pass and the departure chit for the cart.”

“Have you asked to go?”

“Three times.”

“What was the answer?”

“Dole says Harrow will tell him when I'm finished. Harrow says I ought to remember how the rail looked.”

Neri set down the pliers.

Ragna looked towards the porter. He was still talking. She asked, “What do you remember?”

Kelm shook his head. “Not here.”

“Then don't say it here.”

He looked surprised. She kept her hands busy with the bracket.

She took the bracket from Neri and held it above the low heat while Neri checked the wheel. The cart had been dragged with the brake applied. A strip of the rim had been scored, but the axle turned cleanly. Neri could make it safe to move, though Mere would need to decide how much further work was worth paying for.

Kelm held the brazier steady. He could have walked away and left them with it. Instead he waited until Neri needed a second pair of pliers and fetched one from the trolley.

In the lower lane a gate opened. Two carts carrying covered crates came through towards the road. A wizard checked each driver's paper at the top of the slope. Behind them, through the bars at the river, a man led a horse along the towpath. The depot gate stayed open while a porter rolled an empty barrel inside.

Ragna watched until it shut. A means of moving things. Not an easy way out.

Kelm said, “They bring the coal through there. It's wider than the mill door.”

“Do they lock it at night?”

“Yes. And the shed.”

“The shed?”

He kept his eyes on the brazier. “I sleep in the back. I'm not chained. They are careful to tell me that.”

Neri's pliers slipped. The bracket struck the cart with a sharp knock. She swore and picked it up. Her face had changed.

“Leave it another minute,” Kelm said. “You don't want to bend it cold.”

“I know.”

“I know you know. Your father told me you were better with that work.”

Neri stared at him. For a moment Ragna thought she would ask the question none of them could safely answer here. Instead she put the bracket back over the heat.

Dole came out to ask how long they would be. Neri showed him the damaged pin and said it would need replacing. She had a spare of the right size; she wanted the replacement noted on the return receipt.

Dole looked annoyed but wrote it in the margin.

“Don't leave that brazier unattended,” he told Kelm.

“No.”

“And no talk about the inquiry.”

Kelm looked down. Neri looked up.

“He was telling me how the brake had been used,” she said.

“Then he can tell me.”

Dole held her gaze for a moment, then went back to his office. Ragna could feel the hour closing around them.

Kelm waited until the office door shut.

“I have a counterfoil,” he said.

Ragna kept her hands on the cart. “For what?”

“The forty. Received here. They gave me the carrier's part to return with the vehicle. Then the vehicle wasn't released and neither was I.”

“Where is it?”

“In my jacket. They took the pass, not the jacket. It's not a secret weapon. It's a receipt.”

He gave a short, ugly laugh.

“Does it show the earlier twenty?”

“No. Why would it? Different load. I saw that entry in the receiving book when they were settling mine. I don't have it.”

“Can you tell us who signed?”

“Dole signed mine. Someone else signed the twenty. There's a name in the book. I didn't read it for you.”

“I understand.”

“I'm not sure you do.”

He took the warmed bracket from Neri and laid it on the block so she could work it. She struck twice, tested the fit, then struck again. The metal settled into place.

A bell sounded from the office. The porter came to take the brazier's spare fuel back to the store. He put a heel of bread beside Kelm's hand without looking at him.

“Dinner,” he said.

“It's not dinner.”

“It's what there is.”

The porter went away. Kelm broke the bread and put half in his pocket. Neri watched the movement, then looked down at their own bag beneath the cart. She pushed it towards him with her foot.

He shook his head.

“It's only cheese,” she said. “You needn't owe us for it.”

He took the bag. Ragna looked away while he ate. Near the office, a clerk stretched and yawned in the sun. The porter had brought bread. The clerk had a stiff back. Neither would have to do anything more for Kelm to sleep behind the locked shed door again that night.

“I don't want to stay here,” Kelm said.

Neri stopped with the hammer raised. She lowered it to the ground.

Ragna looked at the main gate, the guard, the covered lane down to the river. They had come as two named people. There would be three if Kelm left with them. Their authority would not explain him or make the guard forget he had seen him all week.

“Do you want us to ask again?” she said.

“I want to leave.”

He said it as quietly as before.

Neri picked up the bracket, put it against the wheel and looked at Ragna across the body of the cart.
