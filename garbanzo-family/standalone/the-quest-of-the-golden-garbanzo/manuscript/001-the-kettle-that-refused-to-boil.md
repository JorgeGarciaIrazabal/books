# Chapter 1: The Kettle That Refused to Boil

At seven o'clock on a Monday morning in New Jersey, the Garbanzo family's heirloom copper pot began to rattle like a suitcase full of angry spoons, and a small brass chickpea on four glowing green legs exploded with paper confetti.

"Aha!" cried Garbanzo, hovering three inches above the toaster while his four emerald GPU star-crystals whirred like miniature helicopters. "Morning synchronization complete! Rejoice, Supreme Treasurer! Last Friday you moved five gold pieces from the Checking Boot to the Vault of Distant Horizons, which means, by my superior arithmetic, we are now ten gold pieces richer! Prepare the packing trunks! Inform the king!"

Jorge did not look like a Supreme Treasurer. He wore striped socks, held a coffee mug he had safely avoided cooking with, and stared gloomily at the yellow paper star currently floating down into his coffee.

"Garbanzo," Jorge said, fishing out the soggy paper star with a teaspoon. "You did it again. Moving five pieces from my left pocket to my right pocket does not give me ten pieces. I have the same five pieces and a mess on the table."

Garbanzo's brass eyelid clicked furiously. "False! My emerald crystals never lie! Look at this triumphant upward arrow!"

"Your upward arrow is pointing straight at the ceiling fan," Marissa said, gliding into the kitchen with her dark hair pinned up and two slices of toast that she had very wisely kept out of Jorge's hands.

Everybody in the house knew the Sacred Kitchen Accord: Jorge could wire a garage circuit, build an autonomous mechanical familiar out of spare star-crystals, and tame wild data-dragons across the Hudson River, yet asking him to simmer breakfast was an act of reckless dark magic. Marissa handled the stove. In return, Jorge handled every cable, pipe, and spreadsheet dragon within twenty miles.

Usually the arrangement kept the household humming. This morning, however, the copper kettle at the back of the counter gave another sullen clank and exhaled a puff of lukewarm mist instead of steam.

Sitting by the toaster in purple unicorn socks and mismatched pajama sleeves, six-year-old Clara leaned across her cereal bowl with narrowed eyes. She tapped her spoon twice against the rim like a wizard consulting a cauldron.

"It stopped singing," Clara announced. "Yesterday the Golden Garbanzo was bubbling like soup. Today it sounds like a sleepy frog."

Jorge stepped over to lift the copper lid. Inside sat the prize they had fed for years: a single golden garbanzo, larger than a walnut and warm as an ember, resting in slow-simmer broth. Around the rim of the pot, though, thin frost had crept overnight. It smelled faintly of ozone, wet iron, and office carpet.

"Loom frost," Jorge muttered.

Marissa set down her coffee and reached into her cardigan pocket for a rose-gold compass no bigger than a plum. The moment she laid it on the oak table, its needle swung lazily toward the front door, trembled, and stopped dead.

"And Garbanzo just blasted an early balance ping three hours before his ten o'clock scroll," Marissa said, eyeing the hovering familiar, whose tiny brass nose was still smoking from the confetti burst. "Before you rush to catch the train into Manhattan, tell me honestly. Did you poke the pot last night with another late-night shortcut?"

"I only tested one tiny assumption," Jorge protested, though his ears turned slightly pink. "Just a tiny tweak to see what happens if we retire in January instead of waiting three more winters. And maybe I shifted a few numbers around to make the mountain castle fit."

Garbanzo beeped cheerfully. "A majestic mountain castle! With towers!"

"With a missing roof and four hundred thousand gold pieces in hidden repairs," Marissa replied calmly. She opened a drawer, lifted out a heavy twin-panned balance wrought in dark brass, and placed it squarely between the syrup bottle and Clara's drawing pad.

The Brass Scale of Honest Trade-Offs settled with a solemn gong.

"No more invisible castles, Jorge," Marissa said, not unkindly, yet with the clear edge of a co-captain who had read too many glossy illusions. "Every month we have worked, every trip we skipped, every corner we saved has been warming that pot for years. If we open the Gate of Free Mornings, we do it without lying to the scale. And without leaving me stranded in some lonely woods where the nearest bakery is a rumor."

Clara raised both arms solemnly, her spoon still gripped like a wand. "Also, no boring houses! And I need to know if Poki can find us if we cross the big sea."

The kitchen fell quiet for half a heartbeat. On the windowsill above the sink, a small silver collar bell rested beside a smooth gray stone, untouched since September. Poki had been sleek, sharp-eyed, and dignified right up to the day he walked softly over the Silver Wall.

Jorge swallowed hard and reached out to ruffle Clara's hair. "Poki knows his people, kiddo. Not street numbers."

Outside, the commuter horn blew down the avenue like a distant brass dragon clearing its throat. The Loom frost along the kettle rim crackled sharply, spelling a single mocking word in rime before melting away: *SOON.*

Jorge zipped his satchel, kissed Marissa's cheek, gave Clara's forehead a solemn wizard tap, and caught Garbanzo gently by one warm brass handle before the familiar could launch a second salvo of imaginary wealth into the jam jar.

"Tonight," Jorge promised, one hand on the doorknob. "No shortcuts. Saturday we put everything on the Brass Scale. Even the truth."

"Good," Marissa said, nudging the copper kettle back toward the center of the warm range. "Because the Golden Garbanzo only blooms when both pans tell the same story."
